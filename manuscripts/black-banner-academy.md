# BLACK BANNER ACADEMY

## Working Logline

At the Union's most prestigious orbital command academy, a scholarship cadet from a disgraced border world is invited into an elite crisis-response cohort, only to discover the program is a recruitment funnel for off-book operatives who believe democracy cannot survive unless its brightest defenders learn to work outside it.

## Craft Brief

- Medium: Novel.
- Genre: Military academy science fiction / political coming-of-age thriller.
- Tone: Sharp, tense, institutional, morally escalating.
- Protagonist: Cadet Lio Arden, a tactical prodigy from the failed colony of Vesta Mor, admitted to the academy under a reconciliation quota.
- External want: Earn a command slot and prove that Vesta-born cadets are not liabilities.
- Internal need: Stop confusing excellence with obedience; learn that loyalty to an institution can require defying the people who claim to embody it.
- Opposition: The Black Banner cohort's secret patrons, a faction of admirals, ministers, and covert-service handlers using elite cadets to identify future off-book operatives before they develop public loyalties.
- Stakes: Lio's future, the lives of fellow cadets, the legitimacy of the Union Defense Service, and whether the next generation of officers inherits duty or fanaticism.
- Theme question: What happens when a society teaches its brightest children that being exceptional places them above the rules?
- Ending direction: Lio exposes the recruitment pipeline during a live crisis exercise turned real operation, saving the academy but destroying the career path he wanted and making himself interesting to every hidden service in the Union.
- Audience experience: Competitive academy pressure, friendship under hierarchy, paranoia, tactical problem-solving, moral dread, and the pain of outgrowing a dream.

## Influence Compass

This manuscript should explore the dramatic landscape of a prestigious space-service academy and an elite cadet unit without borrowing any existing canon.

- Academy pressure: Grades, simulations, command boards, social rank, patronage, and mythmaking matter as much as combat.
- Elite cohort danger: The special unit begins as opportunity and becomes a test of whether talent can be separated from entitlement, secrecy, and moral vanity.
- Institutional idealism versus institutional fear: The academy teaches public virtues while powerful alumni quietly sort students for the willingness to violate them.
- Covert recruitment logic: Black Banner is less a unit than a sieve. It identifies cadets who are brilliant, isolated, hungry for purpose, comfortable with ambiguity, and willing to accept secret authority as a substitute for law.
- Cadet command fantasy: Students are given real ships, real weapons, and real authority before they have fully developed judgment.
- Class and colony resentment: The academy claims meritocracy, but homeworld, accent, family service, and political usefulness shape who gets trusted.
- Crisis of oath: The central conflict is not "follow orders or rebel"; it is deciding which oath is real when superiors betray the written one.

## Main Cast

- Cadet Lio Arden: Border-world scholarship cadet, brilliant at asymmetric tactics, terrified of proving his critics right.
- Cadet Amara Quell: Legacy student from a famous officer family, graceful under pressure, privately furious that everyone mistakes her name for competence.
- Cadet Senn Vale: Engineering savant, nonbinary, recruited for systems intrusion after exposing a grading exploit and being punished for it.
- Cadet Rook Sato: Charismatic flight lead, adored by instructors, addicted to decisive action.
- Cadet Iven Marr: Political theory student and Lio's roommate, not selected for the elite cohort and increasingly suspicious of it.
- Commander Talia Wren: Tactics instructor, former war hero, publicly hard but fair; privately worried the academy is becoming a factory for useful zealots.
- Admiral Cael Orison: Superintendent of the academy and patron of Black Banner, convinced the Union's civilian government is too slow to survive the next war.
- Minister Hessa Draik: Civilian defense minister using the academy crisis to test which officers will obey constitutional authority.
- Director Vale Anik: Polished civilian "ethics lecturer" who is actually a covert-service selector evaluating which cadets can lie without enjoying it.

## Causal Spine

1. Lio wins an impossible simulation by sacrificing a flagship everyone else tries to save.
2. Because of that, he is invited into Black Banner, an elite cadet cohort that receives classified crisis training.
3. The first assessment tests not tactics but secrecy: cadets are rewarded for hiding evidence from friends, instructors, and official review boards.
4. Black Banner gives Lio status and belonging, but isolates him from ordinary cadets and normal oversight.
5. The cohort's exercises begin using real fleet codes, live logistics, and political scenarios involving domestic unrest.
6. Lio learns Black Banner alumni have been placed across command posts and covert-service cells as a shadow continuity network.
7. When a border incident triggers a constitutional standoff, Black Banner is ordered to "secure" the academy's orbital yards.
8. Lio must choose between the cohort that finally valued him and the constitutional oath everyone else treats as ceremonial.
9. He turns the academy's own training systems against Black Banner, exposing the pipeline.
10. The academy survives, but Lio becomes a traitor to the officers who could have given him everything.

## Chapter 1 - The Flagship Problem

The correct answer was to save the flagship.

Everyone knew that before the simulation began. It was the kind of answer command schools built temples around: preserve command continuity, protect symbolic assets, maintain fleet cohesion under fire. The flagship was not only steel and thrust and guns. It was the eye of the formation. The voice. The proof that someone still stood at the center and knew what to do.

Cadet Lio Arden destroyed it in the first nine minutes.

The tactical amphitheater went silent except for the weather-soft hum of the projectors. Three hundred cadets sat in ascending rings above the simulation floor, their uniforms dark blue and silver, their academy pins polished bright enough to catch the false starlight. Below them, Lio stood alone inside the command circle while the holographic wreckage of the Union carrier Resolute burned in slow, beautiful fragments around his boots.

An instructor somewhere forgot to breathe into an open mic.

Lio kept his eyes on the plot.

He had not meant to make it beautiful. That was the first thing people never understood about border tactics. Beauty was what core-world observers called violence after distance had made it safe. From inside a collapsing perimeter, there were only vectors, fuel, heat, and time.

"Simulation pause," Commander Talia Wren said.

The stars froze. So did the debris. One shard of the Resolute's hull hung beside Lio's left shoulder, turning lazily in the light, the ship's name split through the middle.

Wren descended the amphitheater steps without hurry. She had the kind of limp nobody mentioned twice. Her dress jacket was regulation plain except for the old campaign ribbon at her throat, a strip of black cloth crossed by three white bars. Every cadet knew what it meant. Every cadet had memorized the battle in first-year history. Very few were foolish enough to bring it up in conversation.

She stopped at the edge of the command circle. "Cadet Arden."

"Ma'am."

"Explain why the Union flagship is currently expanding across six thousand kilometers of simulated vacuum."

A few cadets laughed because they thought they were supposed to. The sound died quickly when Wren did not reward it.

Lio folded his hands behind his back. He knew what that looked like too: provincial cadet imitating command posture, scholarship boy wearing discipline like borrowed formalwear. He did it anyway because otherwise his hands would move, and moving hands made instructors think you were emotional.

"Resolute had already lost primary shields, starboard launch control, and two dorsal heat sinks," he said. "Enemy drones were ignoring escorts and burning straight for her drive shadow. Preserving her would have required the inner screen to collapse inward."

"That is doctrine."

"Yes, ma'am."

"You dislike doctrine?"

"No, ma'am. Doctrine tells you what your enemy expects you to value."

The amphitheater shifted. Not much. Just enough for Lio to feel the temperature change.

Wren's face gave away nothing. "Continue."

He touched two fingers to the control band on his wrist. The plot rewound forty-two seconds. The Resolute reassembled herself, fire becoming armor, armor becoming hull, dead engines blooming blue again.

"At mark eight seventeen, the opposing force committed all drone groups to a decapitation strike. Their cruisers widened here and here, opening a corridor through their own center." Red arcs appeared in the dark. "They assumed I would defend the flagship with escorts, because every command board scenario this term has graded flagship preservation as a priority condition."

"It is a priority condition."

"It was also bait."

"The enemy did not know that."

"They knew us."

That landed badly.

Lio heard someone in the upper rings mutter, "Vesta logic," not quite quietly enough. Another cadet made the soft little hiss that meant shut up, but too late. Lio did not look up. Looking up made it a grievance. Not looking made it evidence.

Commander Wren heard it too. Her eyes remained on Lio. "So you converted the flagship into a mine."

"Into a shaped reactor bloom, ma'am. The carrier's emergency containment fields could hold breach pressure for twelve seconds after deliberate overload. I used the escort screen to herd the drone groups into the drive shadow, then vented the core through the aft spinal channel."

The simulation resumed for exactly four seconds.

Resolute died again.

This time the cadets saw what he had seen. The flagship did not simply explode. It opened. A blue-white blade of contained plasma cut backward through the enemy drone swarm, then through the exposed corridor between the cruisers. Red icons vanished in clusters. The opposing formation convulsed, overcorrected, and collided with its own attack geometry.

When Wren paused the display again, Lio's smaller ships were already moving through the wound.

"Casualties?" she asked.

"Flagship crew lost. Two escort crews lost. Estimated survival of outer convoy improves from twelve percent to sixty-eight."

"You killed eighteen thousand Union personnel."

"The sim killed them when it made the flagship unsavable."

That was the sentence that did it.

The amphitheater did not gasp. Gasping would have been honest. Instead it produced a disciplined silence, which at the academy usually meant everyone was memorizing your mistake in case it became useful later.

Wren stepped into the command circle. Up close, Lio could see the fine burn scars along her jaw, mostly hidden under cosmetic grafting. She studied him the way fleet surgeons studied scans before deciding what could be saved.

"You are aware," she said, "that command is not a game of finding the cleverest way to justify atrocity."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Are you?"

There was no safe answer. The academy specialized in that kind of question. It placed you in rooms where every door was labeled arrogance from the outside.

Lio chose the door that at least opened.

"On Vesta Mor," he said, "we had a saying. If every option is murder, choose the one that leaves witnesses."

The words left him before caution could catch them.

Now the silence was honest.

Vesta Mor was not discussed in the amphitheater except as a logistics case study. Failed evacuation. Command fragmentation. Civilian casualties. Unclear chain of responsibility. A colony whose name had become a footnote officers used to frighten one another into better planning.

Lio had been twelve when the last evacuation shuttle lifted without his mother.

Commander Wren's expression changed by one degree. Not softness. Recognition, maybe. Or warning.

"Reset simulation," she said.

The flagship returned. The enemy returned. Eighteen thousand ghosts accepted their seats and waited for doctrine to decide whether they mattered.

Wren turned to the amphitheater. "Cadet Quell."

In the third ring, Amara Quell stood with the seamless grace of someone whose family portraits had worn uniforms for six generations. She was tall, composed, silver academy pin perfectly aligned at her collar. If Lio looked like he had fought his way into the room, Quell looked like the room had been built so she would have somewhere appropriate to stand.

"Ma'am."

"Take command from mark zero. Preserve the flagship."

Quell descended.

As she passed Lio, her mouth barely moved. "That was either brilliant or disgusting."

He kept his eyes forward. "Usually both."

"That wasn't a compliment."

"I know."

They changed places. The command circle accepted her biometrics and repainted the fleet in blue around her. Lio stepped outside the boundary, suddenly aware of every stare in the amphitheater. Some cadets looked offended. Some looked impressed despite themselves. A few looked hungry, as if he had revealed a weapon they wanted to hold.

In the instructor tier, Admiral Cael Orison watched from behind the privacy glass.

Lio noticed him only because the glass reflected too little. Orison wore the white superintendent's uniform without ornament, an old affectation meant to suggest humility from a man who commanded everyone in the building. Beside him stood two civilians Lio did not recognize: a woman in a charcoal suit with ministerial cuffs, and a thin man carrying no visible credentials at all.

They were not watching Quell.

They were watching him.

The second run began.

Quell did everything doctrine wanted, and for six minutes it was magnificent. She folded the escort screen inward without panic, used destroyers as moving shield planes, rotated damaged ships through the formation, and bought the Resolute enough time to bring two launch bays back online. The cadets relaxed as the shape of the familiar answer emerged. This was what excellence was supposed to look like: disciplined, legible, defensible before a board.

Then the enemy adapted.

It ignored the flagship.

The red cruisers drove straight through the weakened outer screen and opened fire on the convoy the Resolute had been created to protect.

Civilian transports died in batches of white light.

Quell's jaw tightened. She preserved the carrier, then preserved the formation, then lost the thing both had existed to save.

When the simulation ended, the casualty numbers appeared above the floor.

FLAGSHIP SURVIVAL: CONFIRMED.

CONVOY SURVIVAL: NINE PERCENT.

MISSION OUTCOME: FAILURE.

No one muttered this time.

Wren let the result hang long enough to become educational.

"Cadet Arden," she said without looking at him, "Cadet Quell. Report to my office after final bell."

Quell's eyes flicked toward Lio.

There it was: accusation, curiosity, and something worse than either. Recognition.

The class continued. Other cadets took the circle. They saved the flagship. They lost the convoy. They sacrificed escorts too late, retreated too early, negotiated with algorithms that had been built not to listen. Lio watched each failure and felt no satisfaction. Being right did not resurrect anyone. It only made people angrier about what the dead had cost.

At final bell, the amphitheater emptied in disciplined streams.

Lio gathered his slate and kept his pace even. He made it six steps into the corridor before someone shouldered him into the wall.

Rook Sato smiled at him with all his teeth.

Rook was flight-track royalty: bright-eyed, broad-shouldered, famous for breaking an instructor's time record in orbital skimmers and buying drinks afterward for everyone he had humiliated. People forgave him arrogance because he distributed it like sunlight.

"Arden," Rook said. "Hell of a show."

"Move."

"Not criticism. I like a man who can murder a carrier before breakfast."

"I didn't murder it."

"No, you made it philosophy. Much cleaner."

Two of Rook's friends laughed. Lio recognized one from tactical analytics, one from gunnery. He did not know their names. That was probably mutual.

Amara Quell appeared at the corridor bend. "Sato, if you are going to start a fight, do it under a camera with better angle coverage. The disciplinary board hates ambiguity."

Rook's smile widened. "Quell. You here to defend him?"

"I'm here to reach Wren's office, and you are decorating the hallway."

For a second, Lio thought Rook might enjoy that enough to escalate. Instead he tapped two fingers against Lio's chest, not quite a shove.

"See you around, flagship."

He walked away laughing.

Lio looked at Quell. "I didn't need help."

"Obviously. You were about to win the hallway by venting it through a reactor channel."

Despite himself, he almost smiled. Almost was as far as he allowed it.

They reached Wren's office together.

The door opened before either could knock.

Inside, Commander Wren stood beside her desk. Admiral Orison waited at the window, hands clasped behind his back, looking out over the academy's orbital yards. Training cutters drifted in neat rows beyond the glass. Cadets in silver maneuver suits practiced hull transfers across the dark, small as sparks against the curve of Earth below.

The two civilians from the privacy box were gone.

That made Lio more nervous, not less.

"Cadets," Orison said, turning. His voice was warm in the way ceremonial fires were warm: impressive, controlled, and not meant for cooking anything practical. "Today you both demonstrated something rare."

Quell stood straighter. "Sir."

Lio did the same half a second later.

Orison smiled as if he had noticed the delay and approved of it. "Cadet Quell demonstrated command discipline under doctrinal stress. Cadet Arden demonstrated the capacity to identify when doctrine itself becomes the enemy's terrain."

Wren's face gave away nothing.

"The academy has a number of supplemental formations," Orison continued. "Advanced cohorts. Research tracks. Leadership societies. Most are decorative. One is not."

He placed two black envelopes on Wren's desk.

No academy seal. No printed names. Just matte paper so dark it seemed to drink the office light.

Quell did not reach for hers.

Lio hated that he wanted to.

"You are being invited to assessment," Orison said. "Not admission. Not yet. Speak of this to no one. Decline, and your records show nothing. Accept, and your remaining time at this academy will become more difficult, more useful, and less forgiving."

Lio looked at the envelope.

On its surface, visible only when the light caught it, was a banner embossed without color.

"What is it?" he asked.

Orison's smile faded into something more honest.

"A place for cadets who may one day have to save the Union from the limits of its own imagination."

Commander Wren looked at Lio then.

Not warning.

Not permission.

Something harder to read, and therefore more frightening.

Lio picked up the black envelope.

It was lighter than he expected.

That was the first lie.

## Chapter 2 - The Interview Without Questions

The black envelope contained no orders.

That bothered Lio more than orders would have. Orders had shape. You could obey them, argue with them, fail them, write appeals around their edges. The envelope held only a blank white card, a time, and a location printed in ink so dark it seemed cut into the paper.

SUBLEVEL NINE.

0310.

NO UNIFORM.

No signature. No seal. No threat.

The last part was how Lio knew it was a threat.

He sat on the edge of his bunk with the card balanced on his knee while the room's night cycle painted the walls in low amber. Across from him, Iven Marr slept badly under a regulation blanket, one arm flung over his face, slate still glowing beside his pillow with a half-finished essay on civilian supremacy in expeditionary command. Iven wrote papers the way other cadets played combat sims: intensely, competitively, with the private conviction that the stakes were real even if no one else understood them.

Lio should have woken him.

That was the obvious moral answer, which meant the academy had trained him to distrust it.

Speak of this to no one.

Admiral Orison's voice had not sounded like a rule when he said it. It had sounded like a gift. A door opened for you alone. A test of whether you understood that some privileges became worthless if shared with people who had not earned them.

Lio hated how well that worked on him.

He put the card through his desk scanner. The scanner produced an error, then shut itself down. For three seconds, every device in the room went dark. Iven muttered in his sleep and rolled toward the wall.

When the lights returned, the card was blank.

Lio stared at it.

"Subtle," he whispered.

At 0300 he left the dormitory in civilian exercise blacks, academy pin removed, boots soft-soled. The corridor outside First Wing smelled faintly of floor polish and ozone from the air recyclers. Honor boards lined the walls: portraits of captains, admirals, ministers, explorers, and martyrs who had graduated from these same halls and gone on to become statues in other people's arguments.

Lio passed his own reflection in the glass over the Pelagos Campaign memorial and nearly stopped.

Vesta Mor was not on the memorial. Failed evacuations did not become bronze until the blame settled.

The lift to Sublevel Nine did not exist on the public directory. That was almost comforting. If Black Banner had used a labeled door, he might have left.

The service lift accepted the blank card when he pressed it against the emergency panel. It descended without sound past levels he knew, then levels he suspected, then levels that made the pressure in his ears change.

The doors opened onto a corridor too plain to be secret in the theatrical sense. No black walls. No armed guards. No dramatic lighting. Just gray composite, old pipes, and a cleaning drone asleep in its wall cradle with one brush extended like a tongue.

Cadet Amara Quell stood beside the only door.

She wore civilian gray, hair tied back, expression composed enough to be insulting.

"You scanned it," she said.

Lio looked at the blank card in his hand. "You didn't?"

"Of course I did. Mine disabled my room lights and sent my aunt a condolence bouquet."

He blinked. "Your aunt is dead?"

"No."

Despite himself, Lio laughed once.

Quell's mouth softened, then remembered itself. "Sato is already inside."

"Rook?"

"Apparently Black Banner has a charitable interpretation of impulse control."

"Or they need pilots who don't ask what fire is for."

The door opened before she could answer.

Commander Wren stood inside. No dress jacket now. No campaign ribbon. Just a dark field coat and the expression of someone who had objected to a meeting and attended anyway.

"Cadets," she said. "Enter."

The room beyond was smaller than Lio expected. Windowless. Round table. Six chairs. A coffee service no one had touched. Rook Sato leaned back in one chair with his arms folded, pretending not to be impressed by the secrecy. Senn Vale sat beside him, thin fingers moving across a personal slate they had somehow kept operational despite whatever had killed Lio's scanner. Senn looked up and gave Lio a quick, assessing nod.

"Arden," Rook said. "Quell. Welcome to the basement conspiracy."

"Cadet Sato," Wren said.

"Sorry, ma'am. Basement opportunity."

At the head of the table sat a civilian Lio had not seen before: middle-aged, narrow-faced, hair silver at the temples, wearing a brown suit so unmemorable it had to be expensive. No academy insignia. No ministerial cuffs. No visible rank.

He smiled at them like a lecturer about to ask who had done the reading.

"Sit, please."

No one moved.

Wren's jaw tightened. "That was not a suggestion."

They sat.

The civilian folded his hands on the table. "My name is Vale Anik. For the purposes of this assessment, I teach applied ethics."

Senn looked up. "For purposes outside this assessment?"

Anik's smile remained. "I become less interesting."

Rook grinned. Quell did not. Lio watched Wren, because Wren was the only person in the room who looked angry about having a conscience.

Anik placed four slim folders on the table.

"Each folder contains evidence that a cadet at this academy has committed a serious honor violation. Not a prank. Not youthful stupidity. Serious. Career-ending, if referred through proper channels."

No one reached for the folders.

"The evidence is real," Anik said. "The names are real. The violations occurred. You each have twenty minutes to review one file and decide what should happen."

Quell's eyes narrowed. "This is an honor board exercise."

"No."

"Then what is it?"

"An interview."

Rook took the nearest folder. "Finally."

Wren said, "Read before performing confidence."

That earned her a flash of Rook's teeth.

Lio opened his folder.

The first page showed Cadet Iven Marr's name.

For a moment the room lost sound.

Lio read the charge twice before the words became meaning. Unauthorized access to restricted constitutional crisis archives. Copying sealed case law from the academy legal server. Use of a false instructor credential to bypass audit tracking.

Attached were logs. Timestamped. Clean. Damning.

Iven had done it.

Of course he had done it. Iven believed rules were load-bearing structures and therefore could not resist checking whether anyone had hidden rot inside them.

Lio looked up.

Anik was watching him.

There was the interview.

Not the file. Not the violation. The friend.

Quell had gone very still over her own folder. Rook's smile had faded. Senn's fingers no longer moved.

Anik poured himself coffee. "You may ask clarifying questions."

"Why us?" Quell asked.

"Because you were invited."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the academy answer. I assumed you would appreciate brevity."

Rook closed his folder. "If the evidence is real, send it to the honor board."

"That is one option."

Senn looked at him. "What are the others?"

"Destroy it. Bury it. Use it. Warn the accused. Recruit the accused. Blackmail the accused. Contextualize the violation as initiative. Reclassify the rule as obsolete. Protect the institution from scandal. Protect the cadet from disproportionate punishment. Protect your own future by doing nothing traceable." Anik sipped his coffee. "Ethics becomes more interesting when it has verbs."

Wren turned away from the table.

Lio saw it then. The lesson was not what to do with wrongdoing. The lesson was whether they could be made to feel adult by being offered dirty choices.

"This is obscene," Quell said.

"Good," Anik replied. "Start there."

Rook tapped his folder once. "What did my cadet do?"

"You tell us."

"He falsified flight-hour recovery logs after a training crash. Covered for another pilot."

"And?"

Rook's cheek twitched. "And if the board sees it, both are grounded."

"Should they be?"

"Depends why he covered."

"Excellent. You have discovered loyalty. It ruins clean systems."

Senn pushed their folder away. "Mine found a medical implant exploit in the academy infirmary network and used it to alter stimulant prescriptions for six cadets during exam week."

Anik's brows lifted. "Assessment?"

"The exploit is the bigger issue."

"And the crime?"

"Still a crime."

"Which do you report first?"

Senn did not answer.

Anik looked at Lio. "Cadet Arden?"

Lio kept his hands flat on the table. If he picked up the file again, he would read Iven's name until it became a wound.

"Cadet Marr accessed sealed legal archives."

"Why?"

"Because he thinks the academy teaches oath law like a hymn instead of a weapon."

"Is he right?"

"Yes."

Anik's smile sharpened by a millimeter. "Should he be punished?"

"Yes."

"Should he be destroyed?"

"No."

"Should the evidence be reported?"

That was the trap with teeth.

If Lio said yes, he betrayed Iven into a machine that would not care why he had done it. If he said no, he accepted Anik's premise that justice was a private luxury administered by people in basements.

He looked at Wren. "Does Commander Wren know these files are real?"

Wren faced him. "Yes."

"Did she approve using them this way?"

"No."

Anik set down his cup.

The room changed.

Lio felt it before he understood it. Wren had answered too directly. She had broken the rhythm of the test, and Anik had not expected her to.

"Commander," Anik said, pleasantly.

"He asked a clarifying question," Wren said.

Lio looked between them. "So this is not an academy exercise."

Anik's expression stayed mild. "It is academy-adjacent."

Quell laughed once, coldly. "That phrase should be illegal."

Lio closed Iven's folder.

"My answer," he said, "is that I won't make an honor decision inside an unauthorized process run by a man who won't state his authority."

Rook stared at him. Senn went motionless. Quell's face did something almost like approval and almost like irritation.

Anik leaned back. "Principled."

"Angry."

"Often confused by the young."

"Often exploited by the old."

Wren's mouth moved as if she had almost smiled and decided against giving anyone the satisfaction.

Anik gathered the folders with careful hands. "You have all passed the first portion."

Rook frowned. "What was the correct answer?"

"There wasn't one."

"That's irritating."

"It is meant to be. Black Banner does not require cadets who know the rules. The academy has thousands. It requires cadets who can identify which hidden system is trying to make them feel righteous while they break them."

Lio's pulse had started to climb.

There it was. Not patriotism. Not excellence. Selection.

"You are recruiting operatives," he said.

Anik did not deny it.

Wren did. Not with words. With the brief closing of her eyes.

Anik stood. "The Union is defended in public by fleets, laws, ministers, and admirable speeches. It is defended in private by people willing to enter rooms like this and leave without applause."

Quell stood too. "My family has served six generations without needing basement theater."

"Your family has benefited from people who did."

That struck. Lio saw it land.

Anik turned to him last. "Cadet Arden, your Vesta answer today interested several people."

"My Vesta answer got eighteen thousand simulated officers killed."

"And saved the civilians."

"They were pixels."

"For now."

The words hung there, soft and poisonous.

Wren stepped between them before Lio could answer. "Assessment concluded. Cadets return to quarters. You will receive further instructions through official academic channels."

Anik looked amused. "Official?"

"I remain an instructor at this academy."

"For now," he said.

No one moved.

Then Anik smiled again, and the pressure broke just enough for breathing to resume.

In the corridor, Quell walked three paces before rounding on Lio.

"Your roommate?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Are you going to warn him?"

Lio looked at the blank card still in his hand. He thought of Iven asleep beside a glowing essay about civilian supremacy, committing the kind of violation that proved his own point. He thought of Anik offering verbs like knives. Destroy. Bury. Use. Recruit.

Senn answered before he could.

"If he warns him, that becomes part of the assessment."

Rook swore softly. "I hate tests that keep happening after the room."

Quell looked at Lio. "So?"

Lio folded the blank card once, then again, until the paper resisted.

"So we assume everything is part of the assessment."

"And do what?"

He looked down the corridor toward the lift, toward the academy above them, bright with honor boards and sleeping cadets and laws framed in glass.

"Find out who else is being tested."
