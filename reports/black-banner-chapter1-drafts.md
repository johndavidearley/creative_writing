# Black Banner Academy: Chapter 1 Drafts By Option

## Option 1: The Covert Recruitment Pipeline

### Chapter 1 - The Flagship Problem

The correct answer was to save the flagship.

Everyone knew that before the simulation began. It was the kind of answer command schools built temples around: preserve command continuity, protect symbolic assets, maintain fleet cohesion under fire. The flagship was not only steel and thrust and guns. It was the eye of the formation. The voice. The proof that someone still stood at the center and knew what to do.

Cadet Lio Arden destroyed it in the first nine minutes.

The tactical amphitheater went silent except for the weather-soft hum of the projectors. Three hundred cadets sat in ascending rings above the simulation floor, their uniforms dark blue and silver, their academy pins polished bright enough to catch the false starlight. Below them, Lio stood alone inside the command circle while the holographic wreckage of the Union carrier Resolute burned in slow, beautiful fragments around his boots.

"Simulation pause," Commander Talia Wren said.

The stars froze. So did the debris.

Wren descended the amphitheater steps without hurry. She had the kind of limp nobody mentioned twice. Her dress jacket was regulation plain except for the old campaign ribbon at her throat, a strip of black cloth crossed by three white bars.

"Cadet Arden," she said, "explain why the Union flagship is currently expanding across six thousand kilometers of simulated vacuum."

A few cadets laughed because they thought they were supposed to. The sound died quickly when Wren did not reward it.

Lio folded his hands behind his back. "Resolute had already lost primary shields, starboard launch control, and two dorsal heat sinks. Enemy drones were ignoring escorts and burning straight for her drive shadow. Preserving her required collapsing the inner screen."

"That is doctrine."

"Yes, ma'am."

"You dislike doctrine?"

"No, ma'am. Doctrine tells you what your enemy expects you to value."

The amphitheater shifted. Not much. Just enough for Lio to feel the room decide he had become interesting in the wrong direction.

Wren's face gave away nothing. "Continue."

He rewound the plot forty-two seconds. The Resolute reassembled herself, fire becoming armor, armor becoming hull, dead engines blooming blue again.

"At mark eight seventeen, the opposing force committed all drone groups to a decapitation strike. They assumed I would defend the flagship with escorts because every command board scenario this term has graded flagship preservation as a priority condition."

"It is a priority condition."

"It was also bait."

"The enemy did not know that."

"They knew us."

That landed badly.

Someone in the upper rings muttered, "Vesta logic," not quite quietly enough. Lio did not look up. Looking up made it a grievance. Not looking made it evidence.

Wren heard it too. "So you converted the flagship into a mine."

"Into a shaped reactor bloom, ma'am. The carrier's emergency containment fields could hold breach pressure for twelve seconds after deliberate overload. I used the escort screen to herd the drone groups into the drive shadow, then vented the core through the aft spinal channel."

The simulation resumed for four seconds.

Resolute died again.

This time the cadets saw what he had seen. The flagship did not simply explode. It opened. A blue-white blade of contained plasma cut backward through the enemy drone swarm and through the exposed corridor between the cruisers. Red icons vanished in clusters. The opposing formation convulsed, overcorrected, and collided with its own attack geometry.

"Casualties?" Wren asked.

"Flagship crew lost. Two escort crews lost. Estimated survival of outer convoy improves from twelve percent to sixty-eight."

"You killed eighteen thousand Union personnel."

"The sim killed them when it made the flagship unsavable."

There it was. The sentence too true to be forgiven.

Wren stepped into the command circle. "Command is not a game of finding the cleverest way to justify atrocity."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Are you certain?"

There was no safe answer. The academy specialized in that kind of question.

"On Vesta Mor," Lio said, "we had a saying. If every option is murder, choose the one that leaves witnesses."

Now the silence was honest.

Vesta Mor was not discussed in the amphitheater except as a logistics case study. Failed evacuation. Command fragmentation. Civilian casualties. A colony whose name had become a footnote officers used to frighten one another into better planning.

Lio had been twelve when the last evacuation shuttle lifted without his mother.

In the instructor tier, behind privacy glass, Admiral Cael Orison watched without moving. Beside him stood a civilian in a brown suit with no visible credentials. Lio noticed them because the glass reflected too little.

They were not watching the wreckage.

They were watching him.

Wren reset the simulation and called Cadet Amara Quell to preserve the flagship. Quell did everything doctrine wanted. She folded the escort screen inward, protected the carrier, saved command continuity, and lost the civilian convoy when the enemy adapted around her beautiful answer.

The result appeared over the floor.

FLAGSHIP SURVIVAL: CONFIRMED.

CONVOY SURVIVAL: NINE PERCENT.

MISSION OUTCOME: FAILURE.

No one muttered this time.

At final bell, Wren ordered Lio and Quell to her office. Admiral Orison waited there with the brown-suited civilian.

"Cadet Arden," Orison said, "today you demonstrated the capacity to identify when doctrine itself becomes the enemy's terrain."

The civilian placed two matte black envelopes on Wren's desk.

"You are being invited to assessment," Orison said. "Not admission. Speak of this to no one."

Lio looked at the envelope. It bore no seal, only a black banner embossed without color.

"What is it?" he asked.

The civilian smiled as if the question were already part of the test.

"A place," he said, "for cadets who understand that public service sometimes begins where public permission ends."

Lio picked up the envelope.

It was lighter than he expected.

That was the first lie.

## Option 2: The Cadet Coup

### Chapter 1 - The Flagship Problem

Cadet Amara Quell saved the flagship.

That was why she lost.

From the third ring of the tactical amphitheater, Lio Arden watched her command the simulation with the kind of precision instructors loved because it made doctrine look alive. Her destroyers rotated through shield planes on exact intervals. Her damaged escorts withdrew only far enough to vent heat before returning to formation. The Union carrier Resolute, symbolic center of the fleet and official priority asset, survived every strike the enemy drones threw at her.

The civilian convoy died behind her.

White icons blinked out in batches until the simulation floor looked clean.

That was the worst part. Death in training sims became tidy once the system finished counting.

FINAL RESULT:

FLAGSHIP SURVIVAL: CONFIRMED.

CONVOY SURVIVAL: NINE PERCENT.

MISSION OUTCOME: FAILURE.

The amphitheater remained silent.

Quell stood inside the command circle, jaw tight, face composed by generations of officers who had taught their children that shame was a private fluid. If Lio had been in the circle, someone would have muttered Vesta logic by now. For Quell, they waited for the lesson to become elegant.

Commander Talia Wren descended the steps. "Cadet Quell, why did you lose the convoy?"

"Because I preserved the flagship as ordered, ma'am."

"That is not an answer. That is a receipt."

A thin line appeared between Quell's brows.

Wren turned to the rings. "Cadet Arden. Take command from mark zero."

The room shifted before Lio stood. He felt the familiar little drop in temperature that meant three hundred future officers had remembered where he came from. Vesta Mor. Failed evacuation. Border dirt. Scholarship admission. Reconciliation quota, if people were being polite. Charity case, if they were not.

He walked down into the command circle.

As Quell passed him, she said under her breath, "Try not to turn it into a funeral."

"Wrong exercise," he said.

The circle accepted his biometrics. The fleet reset. Resolute gleamed at the center, blue and perfect and doomed.

Wren's voice came from outside the boundary. "Begin."

Lio did not try to save the flagship.

For six minutes he pretended to. He folded escorts inward just enough to look doctrinal. He let the enemy drones believe in the academy's favorite answer. He watched their attack geometry tighten around the carrier's drive shadow.

At mark eight seventeen, he ordered Resolute to overload her reactor containment.

A gasp moved through the amphitheater.

"Confirm order," the simulation demanded.

"Confirmed," Lio said.

Resolute opened like a blue-white wound. The shaped bloom cut backward through the drone swarm and gutted the enemy's exposed center. Lio's smaller ships moved through the rupture toward the civilian convoy.

When the display froze, the casualty report appeared.

FLAGSHIP SURVIVAL: NEGATIVE.

CONVOY SURVIVAL: SIXTY-EIGHT PERCENT.

MISSION OUTCOME: PARTIAL SUCCESS.

"Partial," someone muttered.

Lio looked up. Rook Sato, flight-track favorite, leaned back with a grin sharpened by disbelief.

Wren stepped into the circle. "Cadet Arden, you destroyed eighteen thousand Union personnel."

"I saved fifty-one thousand civilians."

"You were not authorized to sacrifice the flagship."

"No, ma'am."

"Yet you did."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Why?"

Lio could have said because the numbers required it. He could have said because the enemy overcommitted. He could have said because doctrine had become bait. All true. None sufficient.

"Because permission would have arrived after the convoy died."

That sentence changed the room.

Above, behind privacy glass, Admiral Cael Orison leaned forward.

Wren noticed. Lio noticed Wren noticing.

After final bell, Lio, Quell, Rook Sato, and Senn Vale were ordered to Wren's office. Rook arrived smiling. Senn arrived with a slate full of unauthorized notes. Quell arrived looking like she had already prepared three defenses and discarded all of them.

Orison waited inside.

"Today," he said, "each of you demonstrated a different form of command failure."

Rook's smile faltered.

"Cadet Quell preserved lawful structure and lost the mission. Cadet Arden achieved the mission through unauthorized sacrifice. Cadet Sato identified an opening and waited for permission. Cadet Vale found a systems exploit and chose not to report it until after the sim."

Senn looked up. "You saw that?"

"We see many things," Orison said.

Wren's face hardened.

Orison placed four black envelopes on the desk. "The Union is approaching a period in which command delay may become more dangerous than command error. Some cadets must be prepared for moments when civilian authority is compromised, communications fail, and lawful orders cannot arrive in time."

Quell's voice was cool. "That sounds like a lesson and a warning."

"It is an invitation."

Lio looked at the black envelope with his name handwritten in silver ink.

No academy seal. No record code.

"What is Black Banner?" he asked.

Orison smiled.

"Continuity," he said. "For when the system hesitates."

Lio should have disliked the answer.

The problem was that part of him understood it.

## Option 3: The School That Makes Monsters

### Chapter 1 - Highest Proof

Lio Arden had spent three years trying to become the kind of cadet the academy could not dismiss as an apology.

He ran before dawn. He studied after lights-out. He spoke with core-world diction until his jaw ached. He polished his academy pin so bright that the silver bird looked less like an emblem than an accusation. He learned which jokes to ignore, which professors hated border politics, which classmates called Vesta Mor a tragedy in public and a cautionary tale in private.

Excellence was supposed to make origin irrelevant.

That was the first lie the academy taught well.

The second was that excellence was clean.

In the tactical amphitheater, under false stars and three hundred watching cadets, Lio proved otherwise. The simulation gave him a flagship he could not save and a convoy he could not save unless he killed the flagship himself.

So he did.

Resolute died in a shaped reactor bloom, eighteen thousand simulated Union personnel burned into math. The enemy drone swarm died with her. The civilian convoy survived in numbers high enough to make the instructors stop looking bored.

"Simulation pause," Commander Wren said.

The amphitheater froze around the wreckage.

Wren approached the command circle. She had taught three generations of cadets to fear calm voices, and hers was very calm now.

"Cadet Arden. What do you call this?"

"A partial success, ma'am."

"Not a massacre?"

"Also a massacre."

That answer displeased several people more than denial would have.

Wren studied him. "You are proud."

Lio wanted to say no. Pride was dangerous on scholarship. Pride was what legacy cadets had; border cadets were allowed gratitude.

"I am correct," he said.

"Those are not the same."

"No, ma'am."

"Do you know the difference?"

The room waited.

Lio looked at the frozen fragments of Resolute around his boots. "Pride needs applause. Correct needs results."

The silence that followed felt like a door opening somewhere he could not see.

After class, Rook Sato clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to bruise. "That was obscene. I loved it."

"Then you missed the point."

"Probably." Rook grinned. "But I missed it fast."

Amara Quell passed them in the corridor. "You understand that every instructor in the building is going to use you as a warning now."

"They already did."

"No. Before you were a political warning. Now you are pedagogical."

Despite himself, Lio smiled.

It faded when Wren's aide found him outside the mess hall and ordered him to the superintendent's office.

Admiral Cael Orison did not keep trophies in his office. No battle fragments, no antique weapons, no framed citations. Just a wall-sized window overlooking the orbital yards and a desk so bare it made paperwork look morally suspect.

Commander Wren stood to one side. She looked less like an instructor now and more like a witness.

"Cadet Arden," Orison said, "you want this academy to recognize you."

Lio stiffened. "Sir?"

"Do not waste my time pretending ambition offends you. Ambition brought you here. Discipline kept you here. Anger sharpened both."

Lio said nothing.

Orison gestured to the window. Training cutters drifted in neat rows beyond the glass. "Most cadets want command because they imagine command will reveal who they are. You already know. That is why they dislike you."

It was the most dangerous compliment Lio had ever received.

Orison placed a black envelope on the desk.

"There is a supplemental cohort," he said. "Advanced crisis work. Classified simulations. Ethical stress instruction. You will hear rumors. Most are wrong. The accurate ones are incomplete."

Lio looked at Wren. Her expression said nothing. Her hands, folded behind her back, were too still.

"Why me?" he asked.

"Because when the problem was dirty, you did not waste time looking for a clean answer."

There it was: the praise he had wanted, shaped exactly like the thing he should mistrust.

"Is that what the academy wants?" Lio asked.

Orison smiled faintly. "The academy wants honorable officers."

He tapped the envelope.

"This is for what the Union needs when honor arrives late."

Lio picked it up.

The paper was matte black, soft as ash.

For one breath, he felt chosen.

The feeling was warm.

That was how he should have known it was dangerous.

## Option 4: The Rival Protagonists

### Chapter 1 - Two Answers

Lio Arden killed the flagship.

Amara Quell saved it.

By the academy's own scoring system, they both failed.

That was why Commander Wren made the class watch both attempts twice.

The tactical amphitheater had never been so quiet. Three hundred cadets sat in the ascending rings, pretending to analyze the projected battle while actually studying the two people who had broken it in opposite directions.

First came Lio's run.

Resolute died at mark eight seventeen, her reactor bloom shaped into a blue-white blade that gutted the enemy drone swarm and opened a path for the civilian convoy. Eighteen thousand Union personnel lost. Sixty-eight percent convoy survival. Partial mission success.

The room hated it because it worked.

Then came Quell's.

Resolute survived. Command continuity held. Escort rotations were beautiful enough that one instructor whispered "textbook" into a live mic. Then the enemy ignored the flagship, widened through the outer screen, and killed the convoy in clean white batches.

The room hated that because it was doctrine.

Wren froze both outcomes side by side above the command circle.

On the left, Lio's dead flagship and living civilians.

On the right, Quell's living flagship and dead civilians.

"Cadet Arden," Wren said. "Cadet Quell. Center."

Lio descended from the second ring. Quell descended from the third. She wore the uniform like it had been tailored around her family history. Lio wore his like proof of admission.

They met inside the command circle without looking at each other.

"Defend your failures," Wren said.

Quell's chin lifted. "I preserved lawful command structure under fire. The enemy adapted around the expected defense. My failure was not recognizing that symbolic preservation had become tactical vulnerability."

Several cadets wrote that down. Quell had a gift for making mistakes sound promoted.

Wren turned to Lio.

"I recognized the vulnerability and exploited it," he said. "My failure was assuming the right outcome would justify the means to the room afterward."

Quell looked at him then.

Not surprise. Irritation at accuracy.

Wren's eyes sharpened. "Interesting. Cadet Quell, what did Arden understand that you did not?"

"That the enemy was attacking our values, not our ships."

"Cadet Arden, what did Quell understand that you did not?"

He hesitated.

The obvious answer was nothing. The useful answer was something else.

"That command has to survive being explained."

Quell's mouth moved slightly, not quite a smile.

Wren let the silence stretch. "Both of you are dangerous. Arden because he can make atrocity sound like arithmetic. Quell because she can make failure sound like legitimacy. The fleet will offer both of you opportunities to become worse."

The class did not know what to do with that, so it stayed still.

After final bell, Lio expected Quell to vanish into the legacy cadet currents where surnames opened doors before hands reached for them. Instead she waited outside the amphitheater.

"That was almost gracious," she said.

"It was almost true."

"You think I hid behind doctrine."

"I think you trusted it after it stopped earning trust."

"And you think trust is for people who can afford it."

That landed closer than he liked.

Before he could answer, Wren's aide appeared. "Cadets Arden and Quell. Superintendent's office."

They walked together through corridors lined with honor boards. Quell's family name appeared five times. Arden appeared nowhere.

Admiral Orison waited with two black envelopes.

"You each solved the simulation incompletely," he said. "Together, you identified the real problem."

Quell glanced at Lio. "Together is generous."

"The Union will need officers who understand both what must be done and what must be defended after it is done." Orison placed the envelopes on the desk. "Black Banner assessment. Speak of it to no one."

Lio looked at his envelope. Quell did not touch hers.

"Why both of us?" she asked.

Orison smiled. "Because Cadet Arden can do what respectable people deny needing. And you, Cadet Quell, can make respectable people admit it."

There it was. The trap with both their names on it.

Lio reached for his envelope.

Quell reached at the same time.

Their fingers touched the black paper together.

Neither pulled away first.

## Option 5: The Handler's Success

### Chapter 1 - The Flagship Problem

Director Vale Anik selected Cadet Lio Arden seven minutes before Lio destroyed the flagship.

The destruction mattered, of course. Selection committees enjoyed evidence. Admirals enjoyed decisive moments they could quote later as if history had spoken in clean sentences. But Anik preferred the smaller tells: the way Lio ignored the flagship for the first twelve seconds of the simulation, the way his eyes kept returning to the civilian convoy, the way he let the enemy believe he valued what doctrine had told him to value.

Most cadets tried to win the problem.

Lio Arden tried to understand who had written it.

That was rarer.

Anik stood behind privacy glass in the instructor tier while Commander Wren ran the class below. Admiral Orison stood beside him, hands clasped behind his back, white superintendent's uniform severe against the dark.

"Well?" Orison asked.

"Too early," Anik said.

On the simulation floor, the enemy drones tightened around the Union carrier Resolute.

"He is about to lose the flagship," Orison said.

"Yes."

"That does not concern you?"

"Only if he does it by accident."

Below, Lio gave the order.

Resolute's reactor containment overloaded. For twelve seconds, the flagship became a weapon pointed backward through its own death. The drone swarm vanished. Two enemy cruisers collided while trying to escape the bloom. The civilian convoy survived.

The amphitheater went silent.

Anik felt Orison look at him.

"Now?" the admiral asked.

"Now we watch what he does after being right."

Commander Wren paused the simulation and called Lio to account. Anik had read Wren's file carefully. War hero, gifted instructor, principled nuisance. She distrusted Black Banner for moral reasons and protected it for practical ones, which made her useful and unstable.

"You killed eighteen thousand Union personnel," she told Lio.

"The sim killed them when it made the flagship unsavable," Lio said.

Anik smiled.

Not because the answer was good. It was defensive, too sharp, carrying old grief like a blade without a sheath. But Lio did not apologize for seeing the structure of the problem. Shame had not yet taught him to pretend blindness was virtue.

Then Wren asked whether he understood command was not a game of justifying atrocity.

Lio answered with Vesta Mor.

"If every option is murder," he said, "choose the one that leaves witnesses."

There, Anik thought.

Not the sacrifice. Not the cleverness.

The wound.

Selection required ability, but recruitment required hunger. Ability let a candidate do difficult things. Hunger let someone else explain why those things had meaning.

After class, Orison invited Lio to his office. Anik let the admiral speak first. Patrons enjoyed believing they recruited people. Handlers knew recruitment began long before the envelope.

"There is a supplemental cohort," Orison said. "Advanced crisis work. Classified simulations."

Lio listened with suspicion, which was good. Suspicion was more useful than eagerness if properly fed.

Anik placed the black envelope on the desk.

Lio did not take it immediately.

Better.

"What is it?" Lio asked.

"An assessment," Anik said.

"For what?"

"For whether you can tell the difference between an ugly necessity and a beautiful excuse."

Wren looked at him sharply. She knew that line. She had objected to it in committee.

Lio's attention moved to her, then back to Anik.

"And who grades that?"

Excellent.

Anik leaned back. "Everyone you choose not to tell."

Lio's face changed by the smallest degree.

There it was again: resistance.

Anik had no use for cadets who swallowed secrecy whole. They became liabilities or fanatics. The better candidates tested the walls, resented the test, then returned because the room beyond the wall answered something they could not ask in public.

Lio picked up the envelope at last.

Orison looked satisfied.

Wren looked troubled.

Lio looked as if he had already decided to find out where the door led before deciding whether to walk through it.

Anik kept his smile mild.

This was the mistake candidates always made at the beginning.

They thought the test started when they opened the envelope.

## Option 6: The Academy Under Siege

### Chapter 1 - The Flagship Problem

The academy taught cadets that systems failed in sequence.

One shield, then another. One engine, then another. One command channel, then the backup. Failure, properly studied, became a ladder descending toward crisis. If you knew the rungs, instructors said, you could climb back out.

Cadet Lio Arden had never believed that.

On Vesta Mor, systems had failed all at once. Weather, lift capacity, evacuation routing, police cordons, honest speech. By the time anyone identified the first broken rung, the ladder was already burning.

So when the tactical simulation began and the Union carrier Resolute started losing shields, Lio did not ask how to save the flagship.

He asked what else the sim wanted him to forget.

The answer was the civilian convoy.

Thirty transports. Fifty-one thousand simulated civilians. White icons clustered behind the fleet like a school of pale fish behind a wounded predator.

The enemy drones ignored the escorts and burned toward the Resolute's drive shadow. Doctrine said collapse inward. Preserve command continuity. Keep the carrier alive.

Lio let doctrine become bait.

At mark eight seventeen, he overloaded the flagship's reactor containment and vented the breach through the aft spinal channel. Resolute died. The drone swarm died. The convoy lived.

Then the academy's lights flickered.

Not the simulation lights. The real ones.

The amphitheater dropped into emergency red for half a second before the projectors stabilized. A ripple passed through the cadet rings. Instructors looked up from their boards. Commander Wren's eyes went immediately to the ceiling, not the sim.

Lio noticed because people trained for crisis looked toward the real problem before they explained the fake one.

"Simulation pause," Wren said.

The false stars froze. The real ventilation system made a grinding sound overhead and corrected itself.

"Power anomaly?" Wren asked.

An instructor checked his slate. "Grid reports nominal."

Wren did not look convinced. "Cadet Arden, explain your decision."

The class relaxed. Back to the lesson. Back to the safe disaster.

Lio did not.

"Enemy geometry assumed flagship preservation," he said. "The carrier was unsavable under the given conditions. Sacrificing it created a shaped reactor bloom and preserved convoy survival."

"Casualties?"

"Eighteen thousand fleet personnel lost. Sixty-eight percent convoy survival."

"You find that acceptable?"

"No, ma'am. I find it better than nine percent convoy survival with a living carrier."

Another flicker.

This one lasted longer.

The privacy glass above the instructor tier went opaque, then clear. Behind it, Admiral Orison turned away from the simulation toward someone Lio could not see.

Wren saw it too.

"Class dismissed," she said.

The amphitheater did not move.

Wren's voice hardened. "Now."

Cadets stood in confused waves. Rook Sato complained loudly about being denied the chance to fly the problem properly. Amara Quell watched Wren instead of the exits. Senn Vale had already pulled out a slate and was frowning at whatever the public grid claimed not to show.

Lio was six steps into the corridor when the academy-wide address system chimed.

"All cadets return to assigned residence wings. This is a systems drill. Repeat, this is a systems drill."

No one believed it.

Systems drills were scheduled. Systems drills had supervisors at corridor junctions and cheerful reminder banners about orderly compliance. Systems drills did not make senior instructors walk quickly while pretending not to run.

Senn appeared beside Lio. "That wasn't grid fluctuation."

"What was it?"

"A handshake failure."

"Between what?"

"Academy command and orbital yard control."

Lio looked through the corridor glass toward the yards. Training cutters drifted in neat rows beyond the academy spine. Beyond them, drydock arms held three unfinished patrol ships, their hulls open and vulnerable.

Another chime sounded.

This one came from Lio's personal slate.

No sender. No academy seal.

Just three words:

BLACK BANNER ASSESSMENT.

A black arrow appeared beneath them, pointing away from the residence wings.

Senn's slate chimed too.

Then Quell's.

Then Rook's, farther down the corridor, followed by his delighted laugh.

Lio stared at the arrow while cadets flowed around him toward safety.

The academy had given them a disaster before it gave them the invitation.

Or the invitation was the disaster.
