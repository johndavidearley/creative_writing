# Black Banner Academy: Chapter 6 Draft

## The Patron Dinner

The invitation arrived in a white envelope.

That was how Lio knew it was meant to look harmless.

Black Banner had used black paper for secrecy, matte cards for tests, and blank stock for threats that wanted to be mistaken for administrative errors. The white envelope carried the academy crest in blue foil and his name in a clean registrar's hand.

CADET LIO ARDEN

RECOGNITION DINNER

FORMAL ATTIRE REQUIRED

He read it in his room while Iven sat across from him with a book open and no intention of reading it.

"Recognition for what?" Iven asked.

"They don't say."

"Then it is probably for something you did not do."

Lio turned the envelope over. No return address. No seal beyond the academy crest.

"You have a ceremony tonight?"

"Apparently."

"Will there be food?"

"It says dinner."

"The academy calls nutrient paste a meal when a minister is watching."

Lio slid a finger beneath the flap. "Do you want to come?"

Iven looked up.

The question had arrived too quickly. Lio heard the wrongness in it after it was already in the room.

"No," Iven said. "I don't want to attend a dinner for a thing you aren't allowed to explain."

"You could pretend to be interested in the food."

"I could also pretend not to notice that your invitation has no event code, no host, and no list of attendees."

Lio folded the envelope along its existing crease. "It is probably just alumni."

"That makes it worse."

"Why?"

"Because current officers can be held responsible for what they say. Alumni turn into weather. They arrive, change the pressure in the room, and leave before anyone can cite them."

The observation was so precise that Lio almost told him about the profiles.

He pictured Senn's slate. The clean sentences built from ugly facts. **SUBJECT WILL PRIORITIZE OUTCOME OVER PROCEDURE WHEN THE COST IS VISIBLE.**

He pictured Iven's name beneath them.

Not opened. Not yet.

"It is one dinner," Lio said.

"That is what people say before they attend the first one."

Lio stood. "I have to get ready."

Iven closed his book.

"They noticed you," he said.

It was not congratulations.

Lio looked at him.

"What?"

"That is what you want me to say."

"No."

"You want me to be impressed because you have spent three years being treated as though you were one bad result away from proving everyone's suspicion correct. Now a room full of people with medals and private transport wants to feed you, and you need me to say that means you belong there."

The sentence struck close enough to feel like a hand.

"You don't know what it means."

"Neither do you."

Lio put on the formal jacket the academy had issued for ministerial events. It fit across the shoulders and pulled slightly at the wrists, as if it had been tailored for a version of him who expected to wear it.

Iven watched him fasten the collar.

"Do not let them make you grateful before they tell you what they want."

Lio met his eyes in the reflection of the dark window.

"You make everything sound like a warning."

"Everything is a warning when someone else has chosen the lighting."

The dinner was held in the old command museum, a building at the center of the academy that students usually saw only through glass. The museum had been built around the preserved bridge of the Union carrier *Asterion*, a ship destroyed at Pelagos before Lio was born. The bridge had been lifted from the wreck, cleaned, and mounted beneath a domed ceiling where the stars were always visible and never moved.

A string quartet played near the memorial wall.

The instruments were real. The music was not.

Lio recognized the arrangement from a state funeral broadcast.

Quell stood near the entrance in a dark green formal uniform, her hair pinned with a silver clasp. Rook wore black and looked as if the jacket had lost an argument with his shoulders. Senn had chosen a plain charcoal dress and was studying the room's exits.

"You look uncomfortable," Rook told Lio.

"You look armed."

"I am always armed."

"You are wearing formal gloves."

"Exactly."

Quell glanced toward the receiving line. "Three former fleet ministers, two contractors, one current deputy minister, and six officers whose biographies are not available through academy systems."

Senn looked at her. "You checked?"

"I checked the public systems."

"And the private ones?"

Quell's mouth tightened. "Not yet."

A retired admiral approached them before any of them could say more. She had a narrow face, white hair, and the posture of someone who had spent her life making rooms organize themselves around her.

"Cadet Arden," she said. "The boy who killed the *Resolute*."

Lio kept his expression still.

"The boy who saved the convoy," Quell corrected.

The admiral looked at her. "The distinction is why you are here too."

She touched two fingers to Lio's sleeve, not quite a greeting.

"Admiral Serin Kade," she said. "You will sit with me."

"I was told there would be assigned places."

"There are. I assigned yours."

She moved away before he could answer.

Quell watched her go. "That is a good sign."

"Why?" Rook asked.

"If she wanted to insult him, she would have done it where other people could hear."

"You have a strange definition of good."

"It is the one I was raised with."

The tables had been arranged around the *Asterion* bridge. Each setting included a glass of dark wine, a course card without prices, and a small black enamel pin on a square of white cloth.

Lio sat beside Admiral Serin Kade and across from a man introduced as Director Pell, who had no military insignia and an expensive watch. The other seats filled with contractors and ministry advisers. Rook sat beside a flight commander from the outer defense ring. Quell was placed between a deputy minister and an admiral who had served with her mother.

Senn sat at the end of the table near a woman from a private systems consortium.

No one sat beside Wren.

She stood near the museum entrance in her field uniform, not eating, observing the room with the patient dislike of someone attending a meeting she had already lost.

The first course arrived.

Admiral Serin Kade did not touch hers.

"You made an ugly choice in the amphitheater," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Do you know why it interested people?"

"Because it worked."

"No. Plenty of things work. A wrench works. A blunt officer can produce results for six months before everyone notices what he breaks. Your choice interested people because you understood the cost before you made it."

Lio looked at the preserved bridge above them. The *Asterion* command stations had been stripped of every personal mark. The seats were clean. The consoles were dark. It was easier to remember the ship as an idea than as a place where people had sat and waited to die.

"I understood some of the cost," he said.

"That answer is why you are here."

Across the table, Director Pell was asking Senn about systems latency.

"Real command systems are burdened by inherited architecture," he said. "You saw that in yesterday's exercise."

Senn set down their glass. "That was not an exercise."

Pell smiled. "You have a healthy suspicion of labels."

"The system used live traffic."

"Live information is the only information worth training against."

"Then why call it training?"

"Because the alternative is calling it an operation and having three committees decide whether the word is legally comfortable."

Senn's eyes moved across the table to Lio.

Pell noticed.

"You are very good at finding the seam between what a system says it is and what it does," he continued. "Have you considered a career in security?"

"I am in command track."

"Command needs people who can see systems. A pilot can save one shuttle. A systems officer can prevent the shuttle from being placed in danger at all."

It was the first compliment Senn received that sounded like a job offer.

She did not thank him.

At the other table, Rook was laughing. His flight commander had poured him another glass of wine and was telling a story about a rescue run over the Neris shipyards. The story ended with an unauthorized burn and a commendation signed after the fact.

"The fleet is full of rules," the commander said. "The good pilots learn which ones protect people and which ones protect the person who wrote them."

Rook looked toward Lio, then back at the commander.

"And if you choose wrong?"

"Then you make the choice fast enough that people remember the lives you saved."

The commander's smile was warm, but the sentence was not.

Admiral Serin Kade leaned closer to Lio.

"You are from the Vesta districts."

It was not a question.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Your mother worked civilian relief. Your father was a dock engineer."

Lio did not answer.

"You have been trained to hear that as a disadvantage. It is not. You are unburdened by inherited assumptions. When an old officer sees a command structure, he sees the reasons it exists. You see the people trapped inside it."

"Sometimes the reasons matter."

"Of course. That is why you are dangerous rather than merely reckless."

She lifted her glass but did not drink.

"There are people in this room who believe the Union's institutions are too slow for the problems coming toward them. There are others who believe the institutions are the only thing keeping the problems from becoming excuses. You may eventually discover that both groups are right."

"That sounds inconvenient."

"It is the beginning of adult work."

The next course arrived.

Wren remained standing.

Lio watched her speak briefly with a retired officer near the door. The officer touched her elbow. Wren removed his hand.

Director Pell turned to Senn again.

"We have a security fellowship that begins after your second year. It includes access to restricted systems and practical work under supervision."

"Whose supervision?"

"People who understand what you can do."

"People who have read my file?"

Pell's smile did not change.

"Everyone who works in serious institutions has a file. The mature question is whether the people holding it are competent."

Senn looked down at their plate.

Lio knew the silence. It was the silence of someone being offered the thing they wanted by a person they did not trust.

The deputy minister across the room was speaking to Quell.

"Your family has spent a generation proving that command can remain civilian without becoming timid," he said. "You have inherited a difficult position."

Quell's expression was courteous. "I was not aware command was hereditary."

"Influence is. Responsibility is. The distinction is mostly rhetorical."

"That is a dangerous thing to tell a cadet."

"Only if she plans to remain a cadet."

He leaned in.

"Your mother understands the next decade will not be kind to clean procedures. The fleet is going to face emergencies in which the public expects someone to act before the vote is counted. People will need officers who can preserve legitimacy while moving faster than it."

"Preserve legitimacy for whom?"

The minister's smile thinned.

"For the people who still need ships to arrive."

Quell set down her fork.

At the center of the museum, beneath the *Asterion* bridge, the black pins waited on their white cloth.

Near the end of the meal, Admiral Serin Kade stood and tapped her glass.

The quartet stopped.

"The academy does not often celebrate cadets before they have earned the right to be disappointed," she said. "Tonight is an exception. The four cadets at this table have demonstrated a quality the Union cannot teach through standard instruction. They can act when the correct answer has become a luxury."

The room applauded.

Lio felt the sound enter him before he could stop it.

Admiral Serin Kade lifted one of the black enamel pins.

"This is not an award. Awards belong to the public record. This is a promise that the people who saw what you did will remember it."

She crossed the space between them and stopped at Lio's chair.

"Cadet Arden."

He stood.

The pin was smaller than he expected. A black field, a narrow white bar, no crest and no words. Vale fixed it to the left side of his formal jacket, just above the academy insignia.

The metal was cold through the fabric.

"There are moments when institutions must be defended from their enemies," Kade said quietly. "There are rarer moments when they must be defended from their own delay. You may be asked to recognize the difference."

Lio looked at the pin.

He wanted it.

The wanting arrived with such clarity that he almost mistook it for a command.

Vale returned to her seat.

The applause resumed, softer now, private enough to feel like ownership.

Lio sat down.

Rook was next. His flight commander pinned the black bar to his jacket and whispered something that made him grin.

Quell accepted hers without smiling.

Senn held the pin between two fingers before fastening it herself.

Wren watched all four of them.

When the dinner ended, the guests moved toward the museum's outer gallery for drinks. Lio stepped into the corridor to breathe.

Wren followed him.

The museum door closed behind them, muting the music and voices.

"Take it off," she said.

Lio touched the pin.

"It is an academy recognition pin."

"No. It is a marker."

"Admiral Serin Kade said it was a promise."

"Admiral Serin Kade says many things in rooms where nobody is required to record them."

Lio looked through the glass panel at the preserved bridge. Inside, the guests stood beneath the dead ship and spoke about future emergencies over fresh wine.

"You knew this was happening."

"I knew there would be a dinner."

"You knew what they wanted."

Wren's mouth tightened. "They want to see which of you mistakes access for trust."

"And which of us did?"

"You are wearing the evidence."

Lio unpinned the black bar.

For one second, the metal rested in his palm.

Then the museum door opened behind them.

Director Pell stepped into the corridor. He held a second glass of wine and wore the pleasant expression of someone who had never needed to ask permission to enter a conversation.

"Commander Wren," he said. "You are keeping Cadet Arden from his guests."

"He is not a guest."

"Not yet."

Pell looked at the pin in Lio's hand.

"You can return it if you like."

Lio closed his fingers around the metal.

"What happens if I do?"

"Nothing. The gesture will be noted, but nothing will happen."

"And if I keep it?"

"Nothing. The gesture will be noted, but nothing will happen."

Wren looked at him. "That is how they make every choice feel voluntary."

Pell's expression remained mild. "The cadet is old enough to understand a pin is not a command."

"No," Wren said. "He is old enough to understand that commands are not always written down."

For the first time, Pell's smile disappeared.

He turned to Lio.

"Your friend Iven Marr has a strong opinion about institutional accountability."

The pin cut into Lio's palm.

Pell continued before he could answer.

"That is admirable. The Union needs people who insist on records. It also needs people willing to act before the record exists. The mature officer learns to be both."

"Did you read his file?" Lio asked.

"I have not read your friend's file."

The answer was too carefully shaped.

"You know his name."

"Everyone who matters to the assessment has a name."

Pell returned to the museum.

Wren waited until the door closed.

"Take it off," she said again.

Lio looked at the black metal in his hand.

"If I leave it on, what does that prove?"

"That you want what it means."

"And if I take it off?"

"That you still want it."

Wren walked away before he could ask which answer she preferred.

Lio stood alone in the corridor, the dead bridge above him, the pin in his hand, the applause still moving faintly through the door.

He put it back on.

In the museum, the quartet began the funeral arrangement again.
