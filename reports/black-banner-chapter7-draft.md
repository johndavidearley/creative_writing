# Black Banner Academy: Chapter 7 Draft

## Iven's Evidence

Iven did not mention the dinner the next morning.

He mentioned the pin.

Lio had forgotten it was still attached to his jacket until he saw Iven looking at it over breakfast. The black bar sat above the academy insignia, small enough to pass for decoration and plain enough to make the absence of a crest feel intentional.

"That is new," Iven said.

"Yes."

"What is it?"

"A recognition pin."

"For what?"

Lio drank his coffee. The academy dispenser had produced something brown and hot that had once been adjacent to coffee.

"They did not specify."

Iven nodded as if Lio had confirmed a theory.

The dining hall moved around them. Cadets crossed between tables with trays, arguments, assignments, and the low-level panic of people discovering that morning had arrived before they finished becoming competent.

"You are doing that thing," Lio said.

"What thing?"

"Where you pretend not to investigate while investigating."

"I am eating breakfast."

"You are holding your fork like a pen."

Iven looked down. He was, in fact, holding the fork between his fingers and pointing it at Lio's jacket.

He set it down.

"The course registry has a discrepancy," he said.

Lio said nothing.

"Not Black Banner specifically. Advanced Integrated Studies. The course code on your transfer order belongs to a block retired nine years ago. I checked the published catalog, the registrar's archive, and the academy's accreditation records. The code was removed from the active system after the Neris amendments."

"Maybe they brought it back."

"They would need a new authorization string."

"Maybe they have one."

"They do not."

Iven slid a folded printout across the table.

The paper was ordinary. That made Lio more nervous than a secure slate would have.

"Where did you get this?"

"Registrar's office."

"You accessed it?"

"It is public academic information."

"You accessed the restricted archive."

"That is not the same thing."

Lio unfolded the paper.

The first page showed a list of discontinued programs. Advanced Integrated Studies appeared between Orbital Infrastructure Management and Civilian Fleet Liaison, both retired after the Neris amendments. The course code had been struck through in red.

The second page showed a budget transfer.

The numbers were small by fleet standards. Equipment maintenance. Instructor compensation. Technical services. They had been divided across three programs that no longer existed and moved through a sequence of accounts with names like **Emergency Instructional Continuity** and **Specialized Readiness Review**.

The transfers had been approved by different officers over nine years.

One signature appeared at the bottom of each page.

**MARA VENN, REGISTRAR OF ACADEMIC SYSTEMS.**

"She is still in the academy?" Lio asked.

"No. She died seven years ago."

He looked at the signature again.

"The records could have been copied."

"The authorization hashes match the archive's original signing key."

"Iven."

"I know. I checked the key registry twice."

Lio folded the papers back along their existing lines.

"Stop."

Iven's face did not change, but his eyes did. The look was not surprise. It was confirmation.

"That is exactly what you said last week."

"Because I mean it."

"You are in something."

"No."

"You are wearing a pin from an organization you refuse to name, you leave before dawn, you come back with access patterns that do not exist in the academy system, and you are telling me to stop looking at a dead registrar's signature."

"It is not an organization."

"Then what is it?"

Lio looked across the dining hall. A group of second-years at the next table had begun an argument over propulsion theory. No one was watching him. That was what made him feel watched.

"A private cohort," he said. "Command assessment. It is not what you think."

"You have not told me what I think."

"You think everything is a constitutional crisis."

"Only when someone has hidden the constitution."

The sentence landed hard enough to make Lio's hand close around the black pin.

"You should not have these records."

"Why?"

"Because they are not going to help you."

"That is not a reason."

"It is the only reason that matters."

Iven sat back.

The dining hall noise returned in full: trays, voices, the scrape of chairs, the dispenser making another bad decision.

"Who told you that?" he asked.

Lio could have lied. He had become good at it. Black Banner did not require him to invent a story; it gave him a few true details and let him build the rest around them.

"Nobody."

"You just decided that records do not help?"

"I decided you are treating a discrepancy like a hunt."

"I am treating a discrepancy like a discrepancy."

"You do not know what you are touching."

"Neither do you."

Iven gathered the printouts.

"I sent a copy to the civilian oversight clerk before I came here."

Lio's throat tightened. "Who?"

"Mera Sile. Oversight liaison for academy systems."

"You sent restricted financial records to a civilian clerk?"

"I sent evidence that a dead registrar has been approving live program budgets for seven years."

"You don't know that she has been approving anything."

"Then the inquiry will establish who has been using her key."

"Iven."

"No. You don't get to say my name like it is a brake."

He lowered his voice, but the anger remained.

"You are not the only person in the academy who can see a pattern. You are just the only person who thinks seeing it gives you the right to decide who else is allowed to look."

Lio looked at the pin. It was still cold.

"What if the inquiry puts your name on the record?"

"It will."

"What if the people using the key are not interested in an inquiry?"

"Then they have chosen an institution with procedures."

"That is not protection."

"It is better than secrecy."

The words came out between them with the weight of a verdict.

Lio stood.

"Do not send anything else."

Iven looked at him.

"That is an order?"

"It is a warning."

"Whose?"

Lio had no answer that did not belong to someone else.

He left the dining hall before Iven could see that.

---

Mera Sile's account closed at 1014.

Iven sent the notice to Lio in a message that contained no explanation.

**OVERSIGHT LIAISON SUSPENDED PENDING MEDICAL LEAVE.**

The academy system had added a note beneath it: **NO FURTHER ACTION REQUIRED.**

Lio read the message in a classroom while an instructor explained the history of civilian fleet authority. On the wall, a timeline showed the Neris amendments as a sequence of clean arrows from emergency to reform.

The instructor circled a date.

"The critical principle is that military command cannot become a substitute for civilian legitimacy merely because military systems move faster."

Lio looked down at the message again.

Sile had been suspended in less than three hours.

The instructor continued. "Speed is not authority. It is only one quality authority may require."

Lio closed the message.

Across the room, Quell was taking notes. Her handwriting remained calm even when the lecture did not deserve it. Senn sat two rows ahead with one hand under the desk, probably working on something they had not told anyone about. Rook was awake but only technically.

The black pin caught the classroom light.

Quell noticed.

After instruction, she waited for him in the corridor.

"Iven sent me a message," she said.

Lio stopped. "What did he say?"

"That an oversight clerk was suspended after receiving evidence about the registry. He asked whether I knew what Advanced Integrated Studies was."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I did not."

"Do you?"

"I know what it is called."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer I gave him."

The corridor filled as the next class changed rooms. Quell waited until a cluster of cadets passed.

"My mother called last night," she said.

Lio said nothing.

"She asked about the dinner."

"How did she know?"

"Because she was there."

The answer moved through him slowly.

"In the museum?"

"Not at our table. She was in the outer gallery. I saw her leave with the deputy minister."

"Did she speak to you?"

"She asked whether I was enjoying my recognition."

Quell's face stayed composed. Her hands did not. One thumb moved across the edge of her slate, back and forth, back and forth.

"What did you say?" Lio asked.

"I asked her what she was recognizing."

"And?"

"She told me that some programs are easier to defend after they have produced officers people need."

He felt the shape of the sentence. It was too close to Pell's language to be coincidence.

"What did you ask her then?"

"Whether she knew the program lacked authorization."

"Quell."

"She ended the call."

The bell sounded for the next period.

Students moved around them.

"You have been looking into your family," Lio said.

"I have been looking into a phone number in a profile."

"You found it?"

"Not yet."

"Do not contact it."

She studied him.

"You are beginning to sound like Black Banner."

The accusation was mild. That made it worse.

"I am trying to keep you from being used."

"So am I."

They stood in the corridor while the academy bell faded.

Quell's eyes dropped to the pin on his jacket.

"You kept it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He could have said because it was easier than explaining why he had removed it and then put it back on. He could have said because Wren's warning had made the gesture matter more. He could have said because the black bar was the first visible thing anyone at the academy had given him that did not feel like an apology.

"I don't know," he said.

Quell nodded once.

"That is the first honest answer you have given me today."

She walked away.

---

The message from the civilian oversight office arrived at 1840.

It did not come through academy channels.

Lio found it on the black card in his desk drawer, the same card that had once carried him to Sublevel Nine. The screen showed a single line in white text.

**MEETING REQUEST: REGISTRAR ANNEX, 2100.**

No name. No signature.

He carried the card to the service lift.

The panel refused him.

Lio pressed the card against it again.

Nothing.

A voice behind him said, "You have been told not to come alone."

Lio turned.

Anik stood beside the academy's closed honor display. He looked less like a man waiting for a meeting than a man who had been waiting for Lio to decide whether he would understand the invitation.

"Was that you?" Lio asked.

"Which part?"

"The message."

"No."

"Then who sent it?"

"Someone who wants to know whether you are still capable of recognizing bait."

"Is it bait?"

"Everything is bait when someone else chooses the location."

Lio looked at the service lift. "Iven's evidence was sent to a civilian oversight clerk. The clerk was suspended."

"Medical leave."

"You know that is false."

"I know the academy has procedures for personnel health."

"The signature on the budget records belongs to a dead registrar."

"You are repeating a friend's theory."

"Are you denying it?"

Anik's gaze dropped to the black pin.

"You should be careful what you wear in public."

"It is a recognition pin."

"It is an answer to a question nobody asked aloud."

Lio felt anger rise, clean and immediate. He had learned that response from the risk map before Anik had ever said it.

"You profiled us."

"Yes."

The plainness of the answer took the force out of Lio's next sentence.

"You used private information."

"Yes."

"You predicted our choices."

"We predicted some choices."

"And built tests to make them happen."

"You are assuming prediction and design are the same because you want the world to be simpler than it is."

"Did you know Iven would investigate?"

Anik considered him.

"I knew he was the kind of person who would investigate."

"Did you know the oversight clerk would be suspended?"

"I knew the academy would protect its records before it protected a clerk's access."

"Then you knew this would happen."

"I knew the system had incentives. That is not prophecy. It is reading."

Anik stepped closer.

"Your friend has found a real problem. The records are real. The dead signature is real. The unauthorized program is real. If you expose it through the wrong channel, the academy will bury the evidence and call your friend unstable, vindictive, or compromised."

"You sound concerned for him."

"I am concerned for the evidence."

"You don't care about people."

"No. I care about what happens when people are placed inside systems that know how to spend them."

The service lift panel blinked.

This time, the black card was accepted.

The doors opened.

Anik did not enter.

"Registrar Annex," Lio said.

"If you go, you may find evidence. You may also confirm that someone knows exactly how to move you."

"And if I stay?"

"You remain ignorant in a safer room."

Lio looked at the open lift.

"You want me to go."

"I want to know what you do when both choices are useful to someone else."

There it was. The test without a question.

Lio stepped into the lift.

Anik remained in the corridor.

The doors closed.

---

The registrar annex was empty.

Lio found the office at the end of a narrow administrative corridor, past a row of locked cabinets and a dead drinking fountain. The door stood open. Inside, the lights were on. A desk had been cleared except for one folder.

He did not touch it.

The folder carried no name. Its first page showed a transfer history for Advanced Integrated Studies. The second showed the signature of Mara Venn.

The third was a personnel notice.

**MERA SILE — TEMPORARY MEDICAL LEAVE.**

The fourth page was a routing record.

**RECIPIENT: IVEN MARR.**

Lio stopped breathing for a moment.

The file had been prepared before Iven sent the evidence.

At the bottom of the page, a line had been stamped in gray:

**UNSECURED CHANNEL IDENTIFIED.**

He heard footsteps in the corridor.

Not one person. Several.

Lio closed the folder without reading further.

The office door remained open behind him. There was no second exit.

His hand went to the black pin.

The footsteps stopped.

A voice spoke from the corridor.

"Cadet Arden."

Lio looked at the folder, the dead registrar's signature, and the notice that had named Iven before anyone officially knew he was involved.

The voice came again.

"Resolve the channel."

Lio understood then that Iven's investigation had not merely been discovered.

It had been anticipated.

The people in the corridor were waiting to learn whether he would protect his friend, protect the evidence, or protect the program that had taught him the difference was impossible.
