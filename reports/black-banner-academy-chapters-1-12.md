# Black Banner Academy

## Chapter 1 — The Flagship Problem

The correct answer was to save the flagship.

Everyone knew that before the simulation began. It was the kind of answer command schools built temples around: preserve command continuity, protect symbolic assets, maintain fleet cohesion under fire. The flagship was not only steel and thrust and guns. It was the eye of the formation. The voice. The proof that someone still stood at the center and knew what to do.

Cadet Lio Arden destroyed it in the first nine minutes.

The tactical amphitheater went silent except for the weather-soft hum of the projectors. Three hundred cadets sat in ascending rings above the simulation floor, their uniforms dark blue and silver, their academy pins polished bright enough to catch the false starlight. Below them, Lio stood alone inside the command circle while the holographic wreckage of the Union carrier Resolute burned in slow, beautiful fragments around his boots.

"Simulation pause," Commander Talia Wren said.

The stars froze. So did the debris.

Wren descended the amphitheater steps without hurry. She had the kind of limp nobody mentioned twice. Her dress jacket was regulation plain except for the old campaign ribbon at her throat, a strip of black cloth crossed by three white bars.

"Cadet Arden," she said, "explain why the Union flagship is currently expanding across six thousand kilometers of simulated vacuum."

A few cadets laughed because they thought they were supposed to. The sound died quickly when Wren did not reward it.

Lio folded his hands behind his back. "Resolute had already lost primary shields, starboard launch control, and two dorsal heat sinks. Enemy drones were ignoring escorts and burning straight for her drive shadow. Preserving her required collapsing the inner screen."

"That is doctrine."

"Yes, ma'am."

"You dislike doctrine?"

"No, ma'am. Doctrine tells you what your enemy expects you to value."

The amphitheater shifted. Not much. Just enough for Lio to feel the room decide he had become interesting in the wrong direction.

Wren's face gave away nothing. "Continue."

He rewound the plot forty-two seconds. The Resolute reassembled herself, fire becoming armor, armor becoming hull, dead engines blooming blue again.

"At mark eight seventeen, the opposing force committed all drone groups to a decapitation strike. They assumed I would defend the flagship with escorts because every command board scenario this term has graded flagship preservation as a priority condition."

"It is a priority condition."

"It was also bait."

"The enemy did not know that."

"They knew us."

That landed badly.

Someone in the upper rings muttered, "Vesta logic," not quite quietly enough. Lio did not look up. Looking up made it a grievance. Not looking made it evidence.

Wren heard it too. "So you converted the flagship into a mine."

"Into a shaped reactor bloom, ma'am. The carrier's emergency containment fields could hold breach pressure for twelve seconds after deliberate overload. I used the escort screen to herd the drone groups into the drive shadow, then vented the core through the aft spinal channel."

The simulation resumed for four seconds.

Resolute died again.

This time the cadets saw what he had seen. The flagship did not simply explode. It opened. A blue-white blade of contained plasma cut backward through the enemy drone swarm and through the exposed corridor between the cruisers. Red icons vanished in clusters. The opposing formation convulsed, overcorrected, and collided with its own attack geometry.

"Casualties?" Wren asked.

"Flagship crew lost. Two escort crews lost. Estimated survival of outer convoy improves from twelve percent to sixty-eight."

"You killed eighteen thousand Union personnel."

"The sim killed them when it made the flagship unsavable."

There it was. The sentence too true to be forgiven.

Wren stepped into the command circle. "Command is not a game of finding the cleverest way to justify atrocity."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Are you certain?"

There was no safe answer. The academy specialized in that kind of question.

"On Vesta Mor," Lio said, "we had a saying. If every option is murder, choose the one that leaves witnesses."

Now the silence was honest.

Vesta Mor was not discussed in the amphitheater except as a logistics case study. Failed evacuation. Command fragmentation. Civilian casualties. A colony whose name had become a footnote officers used to frighten one another into better planning.

Lio had been twelve when the last evacuation shuttle lifted without his mother.

In the instructor tier, behind privacy glass, Admiral Cael Orison watched without moving. Beside him stood a civilian in a brown suit with no visible credentials. Lio noticed them because the glass reflected too little.

They were not watching the wreckage.

They were watching him.

Wren reset the simulation and called Cadet Amara Quell to preserve the flagship. Quell did everything doctrine wanted. She folded the escort screen inward, protected the carrier, saved command continuity, and lost the civilian convoy when the enemy adapted around her beautiful answer.

The result appeared over the floor.

FLAGSHIP SURVIVAL: CONFIRMED.

CONVOY SURVIVAL: NINE PERCENT.

MISSION OUTCOME: FAILURE.

No one muttered this time.

At final bell, Wren ordered Lio and Quell to her office. Admiral Orison waited there with the brown-suited civilian.

"Cadet Arden," Orison said, "today you demonstrated the capacity to identify when doctrine itself becomes the enemy's terrain."

The civilian placed two matte black envelopes on Wren's desk.

"You are being invited to assessment," Orison said. "Not admission. Speak of this to no one."

Lio looked at the envelope. It bore no seal, only a black banner embossed without color.

"What is it?" he asked.

The civilian smiled as if the question were already part of the test.

"A place," he said, "for cadets who understand that public service sometimes begins where public permission ends."

Lio picked up the envelope.

It was lighter than he expected.

That was the first lie.

## Chapter 2 — The Interview Without Questions

The black envelope contained no orders.

That bothered Lio more than orders would have. Orders had shape. You could obey them, argue with them, fail them, write appeals around their edges. The envelope held only a blank white card, a time, and a location printed in ink so dark it seemed cut into the paper.

SUBLEVEL NINE.

0310.

NO UNIFORM.

No signature. No seal. No threat.

The last part was how Lio knew it was a threat.

He sat on the edge of his bunk with the card balanced on his knee while the room's night cycle painted the walls in low amber. Across from him, Iven Marr slept badly under a regulation blanket, one arm flung over his face, slate still glowing beside his pillow with a half-finished essay on civilian supremacy in expeditionary command. Iven wrote papers the way other cadets played combat sims: intensely, competitively, with the private conviction that the stakes were real even if no one else understood them.

Lio should have woken him.

That was the obvious moral answer, which meant the academy had trained him to distrust it.

Speak of this to no one.

Admiral Orison's voice had not sounded like a rule when he said it. It had sounded like a gift. A door opened for you alone. A test of whether you understood that some privileges became worthless if shared with people who had not earned them.

Lio hated how well that worked on him.

He put the card through his desk scanner. The scanner produced an error, then shut itself down. For three seconds, every device in the room went dark. Iven muttered in his sleep and rolled toward the wall.

When the lights returned, the card was blank.

Lio stared at it.

"Subtle," he whispered.

At 0300 he left the dormitory in civilian exercise blacks, academy pin removed, boots soft-soled. The corridor outside First Wing smelled faintly of floor polish and ozone from the air recyclers. Honor boards lined the walls: portraits of captains, admirals, ministers, explorers, and martyrs who had graduated from these same halls and gone on to become statues in other people's arguments.

Lio passed his own reflection in the glass over the Pelagos Campaign memorial and nearly stopped.

Vesta Mor was not on the memorial. Failed evacuations did not become bronze until the blame settled.

The lift to Sublevel Nine did not exist on the public directory. That was almost comforting. If Black Banner had used a labeled door, he might have left.

The service lift accepted the blank card when he pressed it against the emergency panel. It descended without sound past levels he knew, then levels he suspected, then levels that made the pressure in his ears change.

The doors opened onto a corridor too plain to be secret in the theatrical sense. No black walls. No armed guards. No dramatic lighting. Just gray composite, old pipes, and a cleaning drone asleep in its wall cradle with one brush extended like a tongue.

Cadet Amara Quell stood beside the only door.

She wore civilian gray, hair tied back, expression composed enough to be insulting.

"You scanned it," she said.

Lio looked at the blank card in his hand. "You didn't?"

"Of course I did. Mine disabled my room lights and sent my aunt a condolence bouquet."

He blinked. "Your aunt is dead?"

"No."

Despite himself, Lio laughed once.

Quell's mouth softened, then remembered itself. "Sato is already inside."

"Rook?"

"Apparently Black Banner has a charitable interpretation of impulse control."

"Or they need pilots who don't ask what fire is for."

The door opened before she could answer.

Commander Wren stood inside. No dress jacket now. No campaign ribbon. Just a dark field coat and the expression of someone who had objected to a meeting and attended anyway.

"Cadets," she said. "Enter."

The room beyond was smaller than Lio expected. Windowless. Round table. Six chairs. A coffee service no one had touched. Rook Sato leaned back in one chair with his arms folded, pretending not to be impressed by the secrecy. Senn Vale sat beside him, thin fingers moving across a personal slate they had somehow kept operational despite whatever had killed Lio's scanner. Senn looked up and gave Lio a quick, assessing nod.

"Arden," Rook said. "Quell. Welcome to the basement conspiracy."

"Cadet Sato," Wren said.

"Sorry, ma'am. Basement opportunity."

At the head of the table sat a civilian Lio had not seen before: middle-aged, narrow-faced, hair silver at the temples, wearing a brown suit so unmemorable it had to be expensive. No academy insignia. No ministerial cuffs. No visible rank.

He smiled at them like a lecturer about to ask who had done the reading.

"Sit, please."

No one moved.

Wren's jaw tightened. "That was not a suggestion."

They sat.

The civilian folded his hands on the table. "My name is Vale Anik. For the purposes of this assessment, I teach applied ethics."

Senn looked up. "For purposes outside this assessment?"

Anik's smile remained. "I become less interesting."

Rook grinned. Quell did not. Lio watched Wren, because Wren was the only person in the room who looked angry about having a conscience.

Anik placed four slim folders on the table.

"Each folder contains evidence that a cadet at this academy has committed a serious honor violation. Not a prank. Not youthful stupidity. Serious. Career-ending, if referred through proper channels."

No one reached for the folders.

"The evidence is real," Anik said. "The names are real. The violations occurred. You each have twenty minutes to review one file and decide what should happen."

Quell's eyes narrowed. "This is an honor board exercise."

"No."

"Then what is it?"

"An interview."

Rook took the nearest folder. "Finally."

Wren said, "Read before performing confidence."

That earned her a flash of Rook's teeth.

Lio opened his folder.

The first page showed Cadet Iven Marr's name.

For a moment the room lost sound.

Lio read the charge twice before the words became meaning. Unauthorized access to restricted constitutional crisis archives. Copying sealed case law from the academy legal server. Use of a false instructor credential to bypass audit tracking.

Attached were logs. Timestamped. Clean. Damning.

Iven had done it.

Of course he had done it. Iven believed rules were load-bearing structures and therefore could not resist checking whether anyone had hidden rot inside them.

Lio looked up.

Anik was watching him.

There was the interview.

Not the file. Not the violation. The friend.

Quell had gone very still over her own folder. Rook's smile had faded. Senn's fingers no longer moved.

Anik poured himself coffee. "You may ask clarifying questions."

"Why us?" Quell asked.

"Because you were invited."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the academy answer. I assumed you would appreciate brevity."

Rook closed his folder. "If the evidence is real, send it to the honor board."

"That is one option."

Senn looked at him. "What are the others?"

"Destroy it. Bury it. Use it. Warn the accused. Recruit the accused. Blackmail the accused. Contextualize the violation as initiative. Reclassify the rule as obsolete. Protect the institution from scandal. Protect the cadet from disproportionate punishment. Protect your own future by doing nothing traceable." Anik sipped his coffee. "Ethics becomes more interesting when it has verbs."

Wren turned away from the table.

Lio saw it then. The lesson was not what to do with wrongdoing. The lesson was whether they could be made to feel adult by being offered dirty choices.

"This is obscene," Quell said.

"Good," Anik replied. "Start there."

Rook tapped his folder once. "What did my cadet do?"

"You tell us."

"He falsified flight-hour recovery logs after a training crash. Covered for another pilot."

"And?"

Rook's cheek twitched. "And if the board sees it, both are grounded."

"Should they be?"

"Depends why he covered."

"Excellent. You have discovered loyalty. It ruins clean systems."

Senn pushed their folder away. "Mine found a medical implant exploit in the academy infirmary network and used it to alter stimulant prescriptions for six cadets during exam week."

Anik's brows lifted. "Assessment?"

"The exploit is the bigger issue."

"And the crime?"

"Still a crime."

"Which do you report first?"

Senn did not answer.

Anik looked at Lio. "Cadet Arden?"

Lio kept his hands flat on the table. If he picked up the file again, he would read Iven's name until it became a wound.

"Cadet Marr accessed sealed legal archives."

"Why?"

"Because he thinks the academy teaches oath law like a hymn instead of a weapon."

"Is he right?"

"Yes."

Anik's smile sharpened by a millimeter. "Should he be punished?"

"Yes."

"Should he be destroyed?"

"No."

"Should the evidence be reported?"

That was the trap with teeth.

If Lio said yes, he betrayed Iven into a machine that would not care why he had done it. If he said no, he accepted Anik's premise that justice was a private luxury administered by people in basements.

He looked at Wren. "Does Commander Wren know these files are real?"

Wren faced him. "Yes."

"Did she approve using them this way?"

"No."

Anik set down his cup.

The room changed.

Lio felt it before he understood it. Wren had answered too directly. She had broken the rhythm of the test, and Anik had not expected her to.

"Commander," Anik said, pleasantly.

"He asked a clarifying question," Wren said.

Lio looked between them. "So this is not an academy exercise."

Anik's expression stayed mild. "It is academy-adjacent."

Quell laughed once, coldly. "That phrase should be illegal."

Lio closed Iven's folder.

"My answer," he said, "is that I won't make an honor decision inside an unauthorized process run by a man who won't state his authority."

Rook stared at him. Senn went motionless. Quell's face did something almost like approval and almost like irritation.

Anik leaned back. "Principled."

"Angry."

"Often confused by the young."

"Often exploited by the old."

Wren's mouth moved as if she had almost smiled and decided against giving anyone the satisfaction.

Anik gathered the folders with careful hands. "You have all passed the first portion."

Rook frowned. "What was the correct answer?"

"There wasn't one."

"That's irritating."

"It is meant to be. Black Banner does not require cadets who know the rules. The academy has thousands. It requires cadets who can identify which hidden system is trying to make them feel righteous while they break them."

Lio's pulse had started to climb.

There it was. Not patriotism. Not excellence. Selection.

"You are recruiting operatives," he said.

Anik did not deny it.

Wren did. Not with words. With the brief closing of her eyes.

Anik stood. "The Union is defended in public by fleets, laws, ministers, and admirable speeches. It is defended in private by people willing to enter rooms like this and leave without applause."

Quell stood too. "My family has served six generations without needing basement theater."

"Your family has benefited from people who did."

That struck. Lio saw it land.

Anik turned to him last. "Cadet Arden, your Vesta answer today interested several people."

"My Vesta answer got eighteen thousand simulated officers killed."

"And saved the civilians."

"They were pixels."

"For now."

The words hung there, soft and poisonous.

Wren stepped between them before Lio could answer. "Assessment concluded. Cadets return to quarters. You will receive further instructions through official academic channels."

Anik looked amused. "Official?"

"I remain an instructor at this academy."

"For now," he said.

No one moved.

Then Anik smiled again, and the pressure broke just enough for breathing to resume.

In the corridor, Quell walked three paces before rounding on Lio.

"Your roommate?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Are you going to warn him?"

Lio looked at the blank card still in his hand. He thought of Iven asleep beside a glowing essay about civilian supremacy, committing the kind of violation that proved his own point. He thought of Anik offering verbs like knives. Destroy. Bury. Use. Recruit.

Senn answered before he could.

"If he warns him, that becomes part of the assessment."

Rook swore softly. "I hate tests that keep happening after the room."

Quell looked at Lio. "So?"

Lio folded the blank card once, then again, until the paper resisted.

"So we assume everything is part of the assessment."

"And do what?"

He looked down the corridor toward the lift, toward the academy above them, bright with honor boards and sleeping cadets and laws framed in glass.

"Find out who else is being tested."

## Chapter 3 — A Better Kind Of Alone

The transfer order arrived through official academic channels, which was the first thing about Black Banner that genuinely frightened Lio.

The black envelope had been theater. The dead scanner, the basement, Anik's folders full of other people's ruin: all of it had the deniable texture of things that could be made never to have happened. But the document glowing on his slate at 0600 was real academy paperwork. Routing codes. Registrar seal. Wren's authorization string, valid and verifiable.

CADET ARDEN, L.

REASSIGNED: ADVANCED INTEGRATED STUDIES.

EFFECTIVE IMMEDIATELY. STANDARD ROTATIONS SUSPENDED.

A secret that could produce its own legitimate paperwork was not a conspiracy. It was an organ.

Across the room, Iven read his own slate upside down over a breakfast ration he was ignoring. "Advanced Integrated Studies." He said it the way he said everything he intended to look up later. "That's not in the catalog."

"It's new."

"New this term?"

"New this week, apparently."

Iven's eyes came up. Iven had a way of looking at people that suggested he was checking their citations. "They pulled you out of Fleet Logistics. You're four points from top of section."

"They pulled Quell out of Constitutional Command." Lio kept his voice bored. "And Sato out of flight rotation, which I'm told caused actual grief counseling."

"Sato." Iven set down his fork. "They put you in a program with Rook Sato."

"Integrated. It's right there in the name."

"Integrated with what?"

Speak of this to no one. The rule had been easy to keep for eleven days because there had been nothing to speak of. Now the nothing had a course code, and Iven Marr, who had once filed a formal correction to the academy's own founding charter display, was going to look under it.

Lio thought of the folder in the basement. Unauthorized access to restricted constitutional crisis archives. Use of a false instructor credential. If Iven started pulling at Black Banner's paperwork, he would do it the way he did everything: thoroughly, on the record, and with his own access history trailing behind him like blood in water.

"With whatever the superintendent wants his favorites integrated with," Lio said. "Command grooming. Photogenic cadets for the recruitment feeds. You know how it works."

"You hate how it works."

"I hate it less when it's me."

He made himself grin when he said it. The grin was the lie; the sentence, unhelpfully, was not.

Iven studied him a moment longer, then went back to his ration. "Well. Congratulations." He said it plainly, without envy, which was worse than envy. "They finally noticed."

Lio spent the walk to Sublevel Nine deciding that the conversation had gone well, and most of the day understanding that it hadn't.

---

The room had changed.

The round table was gone. In its place: a full operations floor in miniature: plot table, six crew stations, a command ring with real fleet-standard interfaces instead of the pedagogical versions upstairs that flashed helpful amber when you were about to do something interesting. The coffee service had survived the renovation. Someone had used it.

"You're late," Rook said, from the depth of a crew couch. "By forty seconds. I know because Vale here has been narrating."

"I've been calibrating," Senn said, without looking up from the station they had already half disassembled. "The interface latency is wrong."

"Wrong how?" Lio asked.

"Wrong as in absent. Academy trainers add ninety milliseconds of artificial lag so cadets don't develop reflexes real ships can't honor." Senn turned a panel toward him: a diagnostic tree Lio didn't recognize. "This has none. This is fleet operational hardware."

Quell stood at the plot table, arms folded. "It's surplus."

"It's current issue."

"Then it's a very good training budget."

"It's a budget," Senn agreed, "that doesn't appear in the academy's published accounts. I checked before breakfast."

The door opened before Quell could decide whether to be reassured. Commander Wren entered first, field coat again, a slate under her arm. Anik followed with the relaxed pace of a man arriving at his own dinner table.

"Stations," Wren said.

They moved. Lio noticed how: no shuffling, no glancing. The four of them distributed themselves around the plot table as if they'd drilled it. Eleven days ago they had been strangers who distrusted each other in different dialects.

"Today is not an ethics problem," Wren said. "Today you work." She touched the slate, and the plot table bloomed with a system map: a mid-transit relief scenario, a civilian station bleeding atmosphere, a support convoy nine hours out, and a threat picture that made everyone lean in at the same time.

"Cadet Arden holds the command ring," Wren said. "Sato, small craft. Vale, systems and traffic. Quell, you are the lawful authority on scene: station governor's office. The exercise ends when the station's population is safe, dead, or you are relieved for cause."

Rook cracked his knuckles. "And the enemy?"

"There is no enemy," Anik said, from beside the coffee. "There is weather, physics, panic, three jurisdictions, and a docking queue. You will find these are worse."

It took them six hours.

Afterward, Lio could not have said when the exercise stopped feeling like a test and started feeling like the first genuinely honest thing the academy had ever asked him to do. Somewhere around the second hour, when the station's rotational section failed and Rook flew four evacuation runs through debris that changed vector every ninety seconds, calling his margins in a voice with no performance left in it. Somewhere around the fourth, when Senn rerouted the docking queue through a maintenance protocol nobody upstairs would have graded as legal, and looked at Lio first — not for permission, for confirmation — and Lio gave it, and it held.

Somewhere around the moment Quell, playing the governor, refused his evacuation order in front of everyone.

"Your order exceeds your authority," she said, flat and formal across the plot table. "Station sovereignty is intact. Convince me or route around me, Commander, but you don't get to pretend I'm not here."

The old Lio, the Lio of eleven days ago, would have heard the room deciding he'd been put in his place. Instead he heard the problem. She wasn't blocking him. She was making him better, and she was doing it because she assumed he could take it.

"Governor," he said. "Your station has ninety minutes of rotation left and my convoy has berths for sixty percent of your population. I can't overrule you. But I can tell you which sixty percent boards if you make me choose fast instead of well."

The pause that followed was Quell thinking, actually thinking, behind the governor's borrowed face.

"Proposal accepted," she said. "Boarding priority is mine. Flight plan is yours."

At the end, the plot table showed a station empty and dead and a convoy heavy with everyone who had been aboard it. FATALITIES: 41. Lio stared at the number and waited for the familiar amphitheater arithmetic: the muttering, the Vesta logic, the room deciding what his answer proved about where he came from.

"Forty-one," Wren said. "Fleet solved this scenario in seventy-three, the year it happened."

Rook's head came around. "It's real?"

"Everything down here is real," Anik said. "That is the curriculum."

He walked the length of the plot table, unhurried, and stopped across from Lio. When he spoke it was to all of them, but he did not look away from Lio while he did it.

"Upstairs, you are being trained to be interchangeable. That is not an insult; a fleet requires it. Any competent officer into any competent slot." He tapped the table once, over the dead station. "Down here you are not interchangeable. Cadet Sato flew that debris field because no one else in this academy can. Cadet Vale bent that docking queue because no one else would have seen it bend. Cadet Quell held sovereign authority against a faster answer, which is the rarest skill in this room. And Cadet Arden—"

"Chose fast instead of well," Lio said.

"Chose well," Anik said, "at speed, in front of people, without needing them to forgive him for it." He let that sit. "You have spent three years being tolerated, Cadet. Notice how quickly you've gotten used to being needed. Notice it honestly. It's the most important data you'll collect down here."

The terrible thing was that he was right, and the more terrible thing was that being told the flattery was flattery did not make it stop working.

Wren dismissed them. At the door, Lio looked back once. She was standing over the plot table, the dead station still glowing under her hands, and she was watching Anik with the expression of someone counting exits.

---

They rode the service lift up together, the four of them, and nobody spoke. It was the good kind of silence, the kind Lio had watched flight crews share in the mess for three years without ever once being inside it.

Rook broke it at Sublevel Two. "Same time tomorrow?"

"Every day," Senn said. "The schedule's blocked through end of term."

"Good," Rook said, and then, aggrieved by his own sincerity: "I mean. Acceptable."

Quell almost smiled. Lio saw it happen and saw her decide to allow it.

The doors opened on the residence level, on floor polish and honor boards and the ordinary bright evening noise of eight hundred cadets who did not know Sublevel Nine existed, and the silence changed instantly into four people not looking at each other.

That was the design, Lio thought. Not the hardware, not the real scenario, not even Anik's surgical praise. This. The lift doors as an airlock between the place where they mattered and the place where they were students. Black Banner didn't have to demand loyalty. It just had to become the only room where any of them could breathe.

Knowing the design was supposed to be his advantage.

He kept telling himself that all the way to his quarters, where Iven was waiting up with the lights on and a slate full of registrar records open on the desk.

"Advanced Integrated Studies," Iven said, "has no syllabus, no instructor of record, no assigned classroom, and a course code from a numbering block the academy retired nine years ago."

Lio stopped in the doorway.

Later, he would have time to inventory everything that hung in that moment. The folder in the basement with Iven's name on the first page. Senn's voice in the corridor: If he warns him, that becomes part of the assessment. Anik's verbs, laid out like instruments. Destroy. Bury. Warn. Protect.

And under all of it, small and ugly as a splinter: six hours of being needed, and the cold that came off the thought of losing it.

"You pulled the registrar records," Lio said.

"Of course I pulled the registrar records. My roommate got reassigned to a course that doesn't exist. I have questions. That's not suspicion, that's literacy."

"Iven." Lio shut the door. "Stop."

"Stop what? Reading public records?"

"Stop pulling threads on this. Tonight. All of it."

Iven leaned back slowly in the desk chair. Iven Marr, who wrote essays on civilian supremacy at two in the morning for no audience but his own conscience, who believed rules were load-bearing and checked them for rot because he loved the building. Lio watched him arrive, with terrible inevitability, at exactly the wrong conclusion.

"You're in something," Iven said quietly. "And you can't talk about it. That's fine. Genuinely fine, Lio. Half of command track ends up under some nondisclosure eventually. But if the paperwork is this bad, then somebody built it badly on purpose, which means the secrecy isn't protecting a program, it's protecting a person, and cadets under bad paperwork are the ones who get spent when it surfaces. Let me help. I'm good at this. I won't touch you. I'll go through the records office, formal inquiry, my name on it. Clean."

His name on it.

The formal inquiry would take nine days to route. His access history would be reviewed within one. And somewhere in a basement, a man in an unmemorable brown suit already had a folder that would end Iven Marr's career in a single referral, a folder that existed, Lio understood now with real nausea, precisely for this. Not to test what Lio would do with a friend's violation. To be there, later, whenever the friend became inconvenient.

They hadn't been assessing his ethics at all. They'd been showing him the hostage.

"No," Lio said.

"Lio—"

"There's nothing to find. It's a superintendent's pet cohort with lazy paperwork, and if you file an inquiry, the only thing you'll accomplish is telling every officer who reviews it that Iven Marr thinks his roommate getting picked over him is a constitutional crisis."

He watched it land. He had aimed it to land at the one soft place Iven kept behind the citations, the fear of being the cadet who footnoted other people's careers because he didn't have one of his own. It was the kind of shot you could only make with three years of friendship for a targeting system.

Iven's face didn't crumple. It did something quieter and much worse. It became polite.

"Right," he said. He closed the registrar records, one window at a time, deliberately, the way he did everything. "Congratulations again. On being noticed."

He took his slate and his half-finished essay to the library, and the door closed, and it was very quiet.

Lio sat on the edge of his bunk in the empty room where, eleven days ago, he had held a black envelope and decided not to wake his friend. The room's night cycle came on and painted the walls low amber. Upstairs, eight hundred cadets slept inside a school they believed in. Nine levels down, there was a plot table where he was necessary, where nobody said Vesta logic, where the coffee was real and the scenarios were real and Amara Quell argued with him like an equal and it had taken Black Banner exactly one working day to make that room feel more like home than this one.

He had protected Iven. He was almost sure he had protected Iven.

He had also just, without one order being given, done Anik's work for him: cut the last line between himself and anyone outside the program, and been paid for it in belonging.

All his life the academy had made him lonely in a crowd, and he had survived it by deciding loneliness was evidence of his own clear sight. Black Banner had studied that survival strategy, priced it, and made its offer: not friendship, which he would have distrusted, but usefulness, which he had wanted so long he'd stopped noticing the wanting.

A better kind of alone. Curated. Load-bearing. Shared with exactly four people and one man in a brown suit who collected wounds the way other men collected debts.

Lio lay back on the bunk without undressing and stared at the ceiling.

"Find out who else is being tested," he'd said, in the corridor, eleven days ago, like a plan.

He was starting to understand the answer was going to be: everyone. Including the version of himself who kept score of how good the basement felt.

Especially him.

## Chapter 4 — Live Codes

The next morning, the exercise was waiting for them.

Lio knew because the service lift had stopped accepting the black card.

He stood in the corridor outside Sublevel Nine with the card pressed to the emergency panel while the reader considered him. Behind him, Rook shifted his weight from one foot to the other. Quell had brought a slate and the expression she wore whenever she intended to make a complaint survive contact with authority. Senn was looking at the panel rather than the door.

"It worked yesterday," Lio said.

"Yesterday it was a door," Senn said. "Now it's a question."

The panel turned green, and the lift opened.

Rook grinned. "Good question."

They stepped inside. The doors closed on the ordinary academy corridor, its honor boards and polished floors and students moving between morning instruction with the solemn exhaustion of people who still believed the day's rules had been written before they arrived.

The lift descended.

At Sublevel Six, the display flickered. At Sublevel Seven, it showed no number at all. At Sublevel Eight, the emergency lighting came on, though the lift was not in an emergency.

At Nine, the doors opened on Wren standing alone beside the operations floor.

She had changed out of the field coat. Her uniform jacket was buttoned to the throat, campaign ribbon in place, hair pinned back with the severe precision of someone who did not want the room to mistake her for a conspirator.

It was not convincing.

"You are four minutes early," she said.

"The lift was argumentative," Rook said.

"The lift is not in your chain of command."

"Neither are most things worth flying toward."

Wren looked at him until the joke stopped being one. "Stations."

The operations floor had changed again. The academy's training interfaces had been stripped from the crew stations. In their place were dark glass panels, real fleet-standard controls, and a plot table with no reassuring amber warnings around the edge.

Lio touched the command ring.

The surface woke beneath his hand.

A star map rose into the air. Not the academy's clean blue geometry. This was a living system: traffic lanes crowded with civilian haulers, courier tags blinking in three different priority colors, orbital depots rotating around a brown dwarf whose name Lio recognized from a public fleet bulletin.

PELAGOS RELIEF CORRIDOR.

The name sat in the corner of the display.

Lio felt something inside him close.

"This is not a simulation map," Senn said.

Wren did not look at them. "It is a training environment."

"The Pelagos Corridor is active."

"Many training environments resemble active environments."

Senn leaned over the table. A dozen traffic markers widened as they magnified the system. "Those are current convoy identifiers. The *Morrow* is due through the western gate in six hours. The *Morrow* was delayed yesterday by a coolant fault. That delay went public at 2200. This display updated after that notice."

Rook glanced at Wren. "Is that a yes?"

"It is an instruction to begin."

Quell folded her arms. "Before we begin, I want to know whether this system is connected to any live fleet network."

"You are not cleared to know that."

"Then I am not cleared to operate it."

The room changed by a fraction. Wren's face did not, but Lio felt the shift in the people behind it: observers listening more closely, a test becoming a record.

Wren tapped the slate under her arm. "Cadet Quell, your role today is station authority. You have a civilian relief hub with a failing rotation ring, an inbound convoy, and a suspected navigation fault in the western gate. Cadet Arden holds command. Cadet Sato handles small craft. Cadet Vale handles systems and traffic. You will make decisions with the information provided."

"Provided by whom?" Quell asked.

"By the exercise."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer you have."

Wren turned to Lio. "Begin."

The system clock started.

At first, the scenario behaved like the one they had run the day before. A relief hub lost part of its rotational section. The convoy had enough berths for sixty percent of the population. A debris field was moving through the outer approach, changing vector whenever the station's damaged stabilizers fired.

Lio assigned Rook to the evacuation runs, Senn to the docking queue, and Quell to the station governor's office.

The first difference appeared in the traffic layer.

A courier tag entered the western gate without a transponder handshake.

"Unidentified vessel," Senn said. "Vector toward the convoy."

"Can you challenge it?" Lio asked.

"I can send the challenge through the academy wrapper."

"Do it."

Senn's fingers moved across the panel. The challenge returned with a response code.

She stopped.

"What?" Lio said.

"That's not an academy response."

She opened the packet and expanded the authentication string. The code was a series of letters and numbers, followed by a fleet routing mark Lio had seen only once, printed in a restricted appendix to his logistics course.

"It's a live military handshake," Senn said. "Fleet logistics mirror."

Quell looked at Wren. "Stop the exercise."

Wren stood at the edge of the plot table. "Continue."

"You are using an active system."

"The system is not under your authority."

"That is precisely the problem."

The unidentified vessel accelerated.

Lio watched the projection cut toward the convoy. The numbers were too clean. The movement was not. The vessel's vector shifted in response to the convoy's actual position, not the scenario's scheduled position.

The exercise was looking outward.

"Rook, intercept."

"On it."

His small-craft markers launched from the hub. The vessel did not run. It turned toward the first rescue shuttle, as if it knew which craft carried the lowest thermal signature.

"That's not scripted," Rook said.

"Everything is scripted," Wren replied.

"Then somebody wrote a mean script."

Lio opened the traffic layer. The convoy's public schedule showed the *Morrow* at the western gate in six hours. The live system showed the *Morrow* already three hours out.

He looked at Senn.

She had seen it too.

"The exercise is reading current traffic," she said. "The schedule is stale."

"Can you isolate the live feed?"

"Yes."

"Can you turn it off?"

Senn looked at the unidentified vessel, at the rescue shuttle it was closing on, at the convoy's actual position. "Yes."

The answer was not the one Lio wanted.

"If I isolate it, we lose the current vectors."

"Then don't isolate it. Filter the exercise traffic from the live traffic."

"There is no filter. This was not built to separate them."

The vessel fired a warning burst across Rook's path.

Rook broke formation. His marker slipped into the debris field, too close to the station's damaged rotation ring.

"Rook, abort."

"Can't. If I break now, it gets the shuttle."

Quell stepped closer to the plot. "Lio, the civilian shuttle has no armed escort."

"I know."

"You have lawful authority to order Rook back."

"And then?"

"Then we let the vessel pass while we establish its identity."

The words entered the room and sat there, clean and useless.

Lio stared at the live vector. He could let the vessel reach the shuttle and preserve procedure. He could use the unauthorized data and put Rook through a debris field the academy had not modeled.

The old answer was easier when the people at risk were icons.

"Senn, open the fleet routing layer."

Quell turned toward him. "That is an unauthorized system."

"Yes."

"Lio."

"Give me the channel."

Senn hesitated only long enough to make sure he understood what he had asked. Then they pulled the routing layer out of the diagnostic tree and placed it over the plot.

The system accepted her access.

No warning appeared.

That was worse than a warning.

The live traffic resolved around them. The *Morrow* was not six hours out. It was three hours and nineteen minutes from the hub, running dark on a relief route that had not been released to public systems. The unidentified vessel was not a hostile craft. It was an unregistered tug whose navigation array had failed. Its pilot had been trying to reach the convoy without broadcasting a distress call.

The shuttle was in its path because the tug could no longer see the evacuation lane.

"Rook," Lio said, "the vessel is blind. Do not engage. Push it two degrees port and give it your thermal wake."

"That is not how you push a tug."

"You know a better way?"

"Several. Most involve not being in a debris field."

"Use one."

Rook altered course.

The small craft disappeared behind a cloud of ice and metal. For six seconds, Lio watched only a blank section of plot.

Then the tug marker moved.

The shuttle cleared its path.

The exercise continued without acknowledging the change.

Senn looked at the command ring. "It logged that."

"What did it log?" Quell asked.

"The system just accepted a live fleet override using my academy credentials."

Wren's slate chimed.

She read it once, then looked at Lio.

"Cadet Arden," she said, "why did you access a restricted routing layer?"

The question was almost ordinary. That made it dangerous.

Lio looked at the vessel, the convoy, the rescue shuttle, and the command ring that had accepted his unauthorized choice without hesitation.

"Because the exercise gave us false traffic data."

"The exercise gave you the data available to your clearance."

"Then our clearance was insufficient to keep the convoy alive."

"That is not an answer to my question."

"It is the answer that matters."

Wren's eyes sharpened. "No. It is the answer that works. Those are not the same thing."

Rook's marker reappeared from the debris field, trailing a damaged heat shield.

"Small craft returning," he said. "And for the record, I hate this training environment."

Quell did not look away from Wren. "Who received the override?"

Wren said nothing.

The plot table flashed.

A new message appeared in the lower corner of the live routing layer.

**RESPONSE ACKNOWLEDGED.**

The words vanished before anyone could touch them.

Senn swore softly.

Lio saw the message anyway. More than that, he saw the routing mark beneath it. The same restricted symbol from the appendix. A fleet node had received the override, evaluated it, and answered.

The system clock kept running.

"Continue the exercise," Wren said.

No one moved.

"Commander," Quell said, "we are no longer in an exercise."

Wren's face became very still.

"That distinction is not yours to make."

The next alert arrived before Lio could answer.

This one did not come from the scenario.

The command ring displayed it in white text, without academy formatting or instructor attribution.

**CONTINUITY CELL RESPONSE ACCEPTED.**

For a moment, the only sound in the room was the ventilation system working through the walls.

Rook read the message twice. "What is a continuity cell?"

Senn had gone pale. "A command designation."

"For who?"

She looked at the four of them.

"For us."

Quell reached for the panel. The message disappeared beneath her hand, but the system had already copied it into the event log.

Lio turned to Wren. "Are we operational personnel?"

Wren's answer came too slowly.

"You are cadets."

"That wasn't the question."

The command ring went dark.

Every display on the operations floor shut down at once. The live system, the academy wrapper, the traffic map, the event log: gone. The room returned to its old gray surfaces and the coffee service someone had forgotten to clean.

Senn was still staring at their hands.

"It used my credentials," she said.

"The system used the credentials it was given," Wren replied.

"Who was listening?"

Wren looked at the black surface of the plot table. "You should all go upstairs."

Rook laughed once, without humor. "That sounds like an answer."

"It is an order."

Quell stepped away from the table. "Then issue it through the academy chain."

Wren looked at her.

Quell waited.

The silence lasted long enough for Lio to understand that Wren could issue orders in the basement, through the private channels, using the same authority that had made the live system accept a cadet's override. She could not issue this one upstairs without explaining where she had been and why the cadets were there.

At last, she said, "Dismissed."

They left the operations floor together.

In the service lift, nobody spoke until the doors closed.

Rook leaned against the wall. "So. Good news. We saved the shuttle."

Senn stared at the floor display. "We altered a live fleet route."

"Also good news. Nobody died."

Quell looked at Lio. "You used an unauthorized channel."

"Yes."

"You knew it was unauthorized."

"Yes."

"And you would do it again."

Lio watched the lift numbers rise. Nine. Eight. Seven.

He thought of the vessel turning blind through the debris. He thought of the message in the corner of the display, the quiet acknowledgment from someone who had been waiting for them to prove they could be useful.

"If the alternative was letting the shuttle take the hit," he said, "yes."

Quell's expression did not soften.

"That is why they chose you."

The lift reached the academy level.

The doors opened on students, noise, polished floors, and a world that still believed the difference between training and action could be marked by a line in a handbook.

Lio stepped out first.

Behind him, Senn whispered, "I copied the event log."

He stopped.

She held her slate against her side, face blank, eyes fixed on the corridor ahead.

"Where?" he asked.

"Somewhere the basement cannot reach."

Rook looked between them. "Should I ask what that means?"

"No," Senn said.

Quell started walking.

They followed her toward the morning crowd. Four cadets in academy uniforms, carrying ordinary slates, passing beneath the names of officers who had died for a system that claimed to know where every order began.

Lio looked back once.

The service-lift doors closed.

For less than a second, before the seam sealed, the panel inside flashed a line of white text.

**CONTINUITY CELL RESPONSE ACCEPTED.**

Then it was gone.

## Chapter 5 — Useful Violations

Senn did not show Lio the event log until they had taken it somewhere the basement could not reach.

That was what she called the academy laundry.

The room occupied a narrow annex between the residence towers and the old engineering block, a place with six industrial washers, four dryers, and a permanent smell of hot metal. Nobody from Black Banner came there because nobody with authority did their own laundry. The students who did belonged to first-year flight programs and spent their evenings arguing over machine allocation.

Senn sat on top of a detergent cabinet with their slate balanced against one knee. The machines turned behind them. Shirts and sheets went round and round in the glass doors, blue fabric folding over itself, white fabric vanishing and returning.

Lio stood with his back to the service entrance.

"You said somewhere the basement cannot reach."

"The laundry network is separate from academy instruction."

"Separate isn't the same as safe."

"No," Senn said. "Safe is a word people use when they don't know what else to call a system they haven't tested."

She tapped the slate.

The event log appeared as a narrow column of timestamps and routing marks. The message from the operations floor had been copied, along with the live override, the restricted fleet handshake, and the access trail that began with Senn's academy credentials.

Lio read the trail once, then again.

"It says I authorized the route."

"You did authorize the route."

"I ordered you to open the layer."

"The system doesn't record orders. It records credentials."

The machines thudded through a spin cycle. Somewhere outside, a cart rolled over a seam in the pavement and someone swore at it.

Lio pointed at the bottom of the log. "What is that?"

Senn enlarged the final entry.

It was not the continuity acknowledgment. It came after that, at 0617, three minutes after the operations floor went dark.

**PROFILE EVENT: ARDEN, L. — DECISION CONFIRMED.**

Lio felt the room narrow around him.

"Decision confirmed," he said.

"There are three versions of the same event."

She opened the first.

**SUBJECT ACCEPTED LIVE ROUTING AUTHORITY WHEN CIVILIAN LIVES WERE IMMEDIATELY THREATENED.**

The second:

**SUBJECT WILL PRIORITIZE OUTCOME OVER PROCEDURE WHEN THE COST IS VISIBLE.**

The third:

**SUBJECT REQUIRES AN AUDIENCE AFTER THE DECISION TO CONVERT GUILT INTO PRINCIPLE.**

Lio stared at the last line.

"That isn't a profile."

"No. It's an argument."

"Who wrote it?"

"I don't know."

"You copied the file."

"I copied the file. I didn't copy the person who wrote it."

She closed the three entries and opened a directory beneath them.

There were four names.

Lio's appeared first. Under it, a sequence of dates. The flagship simulation. The basement interview. The station exercise. Yesterday's live override.

The folder had been updated twelve minutes ago.

"They were waiting for the event log," Senn said. "The profile changed after we came upstairs."

Lio touched the screen. The file did not feel real. It was too clean, too complete. Facts joined to conclusions. Private history arranged into a shape that made every future choice look inevitable.

"Open Rook's."

Senn watched him. "Why?"

"Because if mine is a test, his is one too."

She opened it.

Rook Sato's file began with a flight incident from two years earlier. A training shuttle had lost attitude control during a formation drill. Rook had broken the formation and burned hard toward a maintenance spine, saving the shuttle and colliding with a service drone.

The official report called it an unauthorized deviation resulting in property damage.

The private report called it a command instinct under incomplete information.

The academy had suspended the review after three days. Rook's family had paid for the drone and sent a letter to the instructor whose testimony might have kept the review open.

Rook had never mentioned it.

Beneath the report, the profile read:

**SUBJECT WILL INTERPRET DELAY AS A FORM OF NEGLECT. PRAISE INCREASES RISK-TAKING. PUBLIC DOUBT PRODUCES ESCALATION.**

Lio remembered Rook's face in the debris field. The pleasure in his voice when the tug cleared the shuttle. The way he had laughed after Wren shut down the displays.

"He should see this," Lio said.

"Would you want to see yours?"

"I am seeing mine."

"No. You're seeing what they want you to see."

She opened Quell's file.

The first page contained appointment records bearing her family name. Her uncle had sat on the academy's civilian oversight board. Her mother had served as deputy minister for fleet readiness. Three instructors had received promotions after recommending Quell for command track.

None of it proved that Quell had cheated.

The file did not say she had cheated.

It said:

**SUBJECT HAS BEEN TRAINED TO TREAT LEGITIMACY AS INHERITED PROPERTY. WILL DEFEND PROCEDURE UNTIL PROCEDURE THREATENS FAMILY POSITION.**

Senn's own file was shorter.

It contained access attempts. Restricted system paths. An instructor's note from their first year stating that Senn had “an unhealthy tendency to test boundaries that have not yet harmed them.”

The last entry was new.

**SUBJECT WILL EXPOSE A SYSTEM IF GIVEN AN ARCHIVE. WILL PROTECT THE ARCHIVE IF GIVEN A PERSON.**

Senn went still.

Lio looked at her.

"That's not true."

"It's not false."

"You don't know what you would do."

"Neither do they."

The laundry door opened.

Quell entered with her hair still damp from the morning shower, a uniform jacket folded over one arm. She took in the machines, Senn on the cabinet, Lio at the service entrance, and the look on both their faces.

"What happened?"

Senn held out the slate.

Quell read her own file without changing expression. She reached the line about inherited legitimacy and read it twice.

"My mother knew," she said.

It was not a question.

"You don't know that," Lio said.

"No. I know she would use the phrase ‘inherited legitimacy’ if she wanted to insult me at breakfast."

She scrolled.

At the bottom of her file was a list of “stabilization contacts.” Two names belonged to retired officers. One belonged to a ministerial aide. The last was a private number with no name attached.

Quell copied it onto her own slate.

"Don't contact it," Senn said.

"I wasn't planning to."

"You were thinking about it."

"Those are different things."

The door opened again.

Anik stepped inside.

He wore a brown suit that did not look expensive until you noticed the absence of every ordinary flaw. No lint. No creasing at the elbows. No shine where a hand had rested against the fabric. He closed the door behind him and glanced at the turning machines.

"Clean place for dirty information," he said.

Rook appeared behind him, carrying two paper cups and looking annoyed at the fact that he had been made to walk through half the academy for coffee.

"You could have told me we were meeting in laundry," he said. "I would have worn something with pockets."

Anik looked at the slate in Senn's hand. "You found the risk maps."

Quell's gaze hardened. "You call them that?"

"The files are not personnel dossiers. They do not contain disciplinary authority, medical orders, or formal assessments. They are maps of pressure."

"You catalogued private information about cadets without consent."

"So does every command organization that has survived more than one war. We are simply less sentimental about pretending the information does not exist."

Rook set the coffee on top of a washer. "My file says praise makes me reckless."

"Does it?"

"You tell me."

"I am not the person who wrote the observation."

"You know who did."

Anik gave him a mild look. "Knowing is not the same as telling."

Rook took one of the coffees and drank from it. His hand was steady.

That was the part Lio disliked. Rook was angry, but the anger had nowhere to go. There was no officer to confront, no rule to cite, no door he could kick open and call the matter settled. The file had turned his own history into an instrument and left him holding the handle.

Quell stepped off the cabinet. "Delete them."

Anik smiled faintly. "Which one?"

"All of them."

"That is not how archives work."

"You are not an archive. You are a man hiding behind one."

"Sometimes the distinction is administrative."

Senn looked up from the slate. "The profiles contain predictions."

Anik said nothing.

"Not assessments. Predictions. They describe choices we have not made."

"Predictions are assessments with better grammar."

"You wrote that I will protect an archive if given a person."

"Will you?"

Senn's face became unreadable.

Anik turned to Lio. "The program does not need you to like the profiles. It needs you to understand that command is pressure applied to incomplete people. Your officers will carry debts, loyalties, illnesses, resentments, appetites, and private definitions of shame into every decision they make. If you refuse to know those things, you are not protecting their dignity. You are surrendering your judgment to the first person who does know them."

"That makes violation sound like responsibility," Lio said.

"Violation is sometimes the cost of responsibility."

"And who decides when the cost is acceptable?"

"The person who is still there after the decision."

Lio had heard versions of that answer from the academy, from fleet officers, from people who believed survival was proof of wisdom. It was a sentence built to survive its own consequences.

Anik reached into his jacket and removed a small black drive.

He placed it on the washer beside Rook's coffee.

"One file can be deleted."

Nobody moved.

"Choose," Anik said. "The system will record the request, but not the reason."

Rook looked at his own folder. Quell looked at hers. Senn looked at the drive.

Lio understood the trap immediately.

It was not asking which person mattered most. It was asking which vulnerability the cohort could bear to leave in the system. The choice would reveal hierarchy, fear, resentment, and the exact shape of their loyalty.

The machine behind Anik began its final spin cycle.

"No," Lio said.

Anik looked at him. "No?"

"We are not choosing."

"Then no file is deleted."

"Correct."

Rook put down his coffee. "I agree with Arden."

Quell's eyes stayed on the black drive. "So do I."

Senn reached for it.

Lio turned. "What are you doing?"

She picked up the drive and held it out to Anik.

"You said one file can be deleted."

Anik accepted it.

Senn's voice was quiet. "Delete the file on the person most likely to compromise the cohort."

Rook stared at her. Quell's expression tightened.

"You don't know who that is," Lio said.

"Exactly."

Anik looked at the four of them. For the first time since Lio had met him, his face showed something like pleasure without disguise.

"Good," he said.

He turned the drive once between his fingers.

"You have understood the assignment."

"We refused the assignment," Quell said.

"That is a distinction people make when they want credit for the result without admitting they participated in the test."

He put the drive back in his pocket.

"Your files remain."

Rook swore.

Anik opened the door. "One more thing. The profiles are not accurate because we have perfect information. They are accurate because you keep giving us better information."

He left.

The door closed.

The washers continued turning.

Senn waited until the sound of his footsteps disappeared beyond the engineering block. Then they climbed down from the cabinet and removed the slate's memory wafer.

Quell looked at it. "What is that?"

"A copy."

"You said the event log was the copy."

"That was the copy I wanted Lio to know about."

Lio felt the old room inside him, the one Black Banner had made, tilt by a degree.

"Where is the other one?" he asked.

Senn slid the wafer into the lining of their sleeve. "Somewhere the basement cannot reach."

Rook picked up his coffee. It had gone cold.

"My file says I escalate when people doubt me."

"Do you?" Quell asked.

"I don't know."

He looked toward the door Anik had used.

"But I know I want to hit him."

"That may be the same thing," Senn said.

Rook considered this. "It isn't helping."

Quell folded her jacket over her arm. "My family may have known about Black Banner."

Lio looked at her.

"You said that already."

"I am going to find out whether it is true."

"Without contacting the number."

"I did not say I would contact the number."

The answer was careful enough to be a lie or a promise.

They left the laundry together.

In the corridor, the academy was beginning its first bell. Students streamed toward instruction in uniforms that marked them as equal from a distance and sorted them by history up close.

Lio walked beside Senn.

"What does your file say about me?" he asked.

"Which part?"

"The part you didn't show me."

She looked at him then.

"You assume there is a part."

"There is always a part."

"That is one of the things they wrote about you."

"What thing?"

"That you treat suspicion as evidence of intelligence."

He almost laughed.

The line was good. That was the problem. It sounded true because it had been shaped around something real.

"What did they write about Iven?" he asked.

Senn's expression changed.

Not fear. Calculation.

"I haven't opened his file."

"There is one?"

"There are files for everyone who matters to the assessment."

They reached the stairwell.

For a moment, Lio imagined taking the slate back from her, finding Iven's name, and reading every line. He imagined knowing what Black Banner knew. He imagined using that knowledge to protect his friend before the program could turn it into a weapon.

He also imagined what it would mean to open the file.

The door to the stairwell waited between them.

"Don't open it," he said.

Senn nodded.

"Why?"

"Because the next time they offer us a moral choice, the choice itself will be evidence."

"That sounds like something they would say."

"I know."

They entered the stairwell.

Behind them, the academy bell began to ring.

Lio climbed toward class with the file on his own life somewhere inside Senn's sleeve, the profile of Rook's buried accident still bright in his mind, and the uncomfortable knowledge that refusing a test did not mean escaping it.

It only gave the people watching him a different answer to record.

## Chapter 6 — The Patron Dinner

The invitation arrived in a white envelope.

That was how Lio knew it was meant to look harmless.

Black Banner had used black paper for secrecy, matte cards for tests, and blank stock for threats that wanted to be mistaken for administrative errors. The white envelope carried the academy crest in blue foil and his name in a clean registrar's hand.

CADET LIO ARDEN

RECOGNITION DINNER

FORMAL ATTIRE REQUIRED

He read it in his room while Iven sat across from him with a book open and no intention of reading it.

"Recognition for what?" Iven asked.

"They don't say."

"Then it is probably for something you did not do."

Lio turned the envelope over. No return address. No seal beyond the academy crest.

"You have a ceremony tonight?"

"Apparently."

"Will there be food?"

"It says dinner."

"The academy calls nutrient paste a meal when a minister is watching."

Lio slid a finger beneath the flap. "Do you want to come?"

Iven looked up.

The question had arrived too quickly. Lio heard the wrongness in it after it was already in the room.

"No," Iven said. "I don't want to attend a dinner for a thing you aren't allowed to explain."

"You could pretend to be interested in the food."

"I could also pretend not to notice that your invitation has no event code, no host, and no list of attendees."

Lio folded the envelope along its existing crease. "It is probably just alumni."

"That makes it worse."

"Why?"

"Because current officers can be held responsible for what they say. Alumni turn into weather. They arrive, change the pressure in the room, and leave before anyone can cite them."

The observation was so precise that Lio almost told him about the profiles.

He pictured Senn's slate. The clean sentences built from ugly facts. **SUBJECT WILL PRIORITIZE OUTCOME OVER PROCEDURE WHEN THE COST IS VISIBLE.**

He pictured Iven's name beneath them.

Not opened. Not yet.

"It is one dinner," Lio said.

"That is what people say before they attend the first one."

Lio stood. "I have to get ready."

Iven closed his book.

"They noticed you," he said.

It was not congratulations.

Lio looked at him.

"What?"

"That is what you want me to say."

"No."

"You want me to be impressed because you have spent three years being treated as though you were one bad result away from proving everyone's suspicion correct. Now a room full of people with medals and private transport wants to feed you, and you need me to say that means you belong there."

The sentence struck close enough to feel like a hand.

"You don't know what it means."

"Neither do you."

Lio put on the formal jacket the academy had issued for ministerial events. It fit across the shoulders and pulled slightly at the wrists, as if it had been tailored for a version of him who expected to wear it.

Iven watched him fasten the collar.

"Do not let them make you grateful before they tell you what they want."

Lio met his eyes in the reflection of the dark window.

"You make everything sound like a warning."

"Everything is a warning when someone else has chosen the lighting."

The dinner was held in the old command museum, a building at the center of the academy that students usually saw only through glass. The museum had been built around the preserved bridge of the Union carrier *Asterion*, a ship destroyed at Pelagos before Lio was born. The bridge had been lifted from the wreck, cleaned, and mounted beneath a domed ceiling where the stars were always visible and never moved.

A string quartet played near the memorial wall.

The instruments were real. The music was not.

Lio recognized the arrangement from a state funeral broadcast.

Quell stood near the entrance in a dark green formal uniform, her hair pinned with a silver clasp. Rook wore black and looked as if the jacket had lost an argument with his shoulders. Senn had chosen a plain charcoal dress and was studying the room's exits.

"You look uncomfortable," Rook told Lio.

"You look armed."

"I am always armed."

"You are wearing formal gloves."

"Exactly."

Quell glanced toward the receiving line. "Three former fleet ministers, two contractors, one current deputy minister, and six officers whose biographies are not available through academy systems."

Senn looked at her. "You checked?"

"I checked the public systems."

"And the private ones?"

Quell's mouth tightened. "Not yet."

A retired admiral approached them before any of them could say more. She had a narrow face, white hair, and the posture of someone who had spent her life making rooms organize themselves around her.

"Cadet Arden," she said. "The boy who killed the *Resolute*."

Lio kept his expression still.

"The boy who saved the convoy," Quell corrected.

The admiral looked at her. "The distinction is why you are here too."

She touched two fingers to Lio's sleeve, not quite a greeting.

"Admiral Serin Kade," she said. "You will sit with me."

"I was told there would be assigned places."

"There are. I assigned yours."

She moved away before he could answer.

Quell watched her go. "That is a good sign."

"Why?" Rook asked.

"If she wanted to insult him, she would have done it where other people could hear."

"You have a strange definition of good."

"It is the one I was raised with."

The tables had been arranged around the *Asterion* bridge. Each setting included a glass of dark wine, a course card without prices, and a small black enamel pin on a square of white cloth.

Lio sat beside Admiral Serin Kade and across from a man introduced as Director Pell, who had no military insignia and an expensive watch. The other seats filled with contractors and ministry advisers. Rook sat beside a flight commander from the outer defense ring. Quell was placed between a deputy minister and an admiral who had served with her mother.

Senn sat at the end of the table near a woman from a private systems consortium.

No one sat beside Wren.

She stood near the museum entrance in her field uniform, not eating, observing the room with the patient dislike of someone attending a meeting she had already lost.

The first course arrived.

Admiral Serin Kade did not touch hers.

"You made an ugly choice in the amphitheater," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Do you know why it interested people?"

"Because it worked."

"No. Plenty of things work. A wrench works. A blunt officer can produce results for six months before everyone notices what he breaks. Your choice interested people because you understood the cost before you made it."

Lio looked at the preserved bridge above them. The *Asterion* command stations had been stripped of every personal mark. The seats were clean. The consoles were dark. It was easier to remember the ship as an idea than as a place where people had sat and waited to die.

"I understood some of the cost," he said.

"That answer is why you are here."

Across the table, Director Pell was asking Senn about systems latency.

"Real command systems are burdened by inherited architecture," he said. "You saw that in yesterday's exercise."

Senn set down their glass. "That was not an exercise."

Pell smiled. "You have a healthy suspicion of labels."

"The system used live traffic."

"Live information is the only information worth training against."

"Then why call it training?"

"Because the alternative is calling it an operation and having three committees decide whether the word is legally comfortable."

Senn's eyes moved across the table to Lio.

Pell noticed.

"You are very good at finding the seam between what a system says it is and what it does," he continued. "Have you considered a career in security?"

"I am in command track."

"Command needs people who can see systems. A pilot can save one shuttle. A systems officer can prevent the shuttle from being placed in danger at all."

It was the first compliment Senn received that sounded like a job offer.

She did not thank him.

At the other table, Rook was laughing. His flight commander had poured him another glass of wine and was telling a story about a rescue run over the Neris shipyards. The story ended with an unauthorized burn and a commendation signed after the fact.

"The fleet is full of rules," the commander said. "The good pilots learn which ones protect people and which ones protect the person who wrote them."

Rook looked toward Lio, then back at the commander.

"And if you choose wrong?"

"Then you make the choice fast enough that people remember the lives you saved."

The commander's smile was warm, but the sentence was not.

Admiral Serin Kade leaned closer to Lio.

"You are from the Vesta districts."

It was not a question.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Your mother worked civilian relief. Your father was a dock engineer."

Lio did not answer.

"You have been trained to hear that as a disadvantage. It is not. You are unburdened by inherited assumptions. When an old officer sees a command structure, he sees the reasons it exists. You see the people trapped inside it."

"Sometimes the reasons matter."

"Of course. That is why you are dangerous rather than merely reckless."

She lifted her glass but did not drink.

"There are people in this room who believe the Union's institutions are too slow for the problems coming toward them. There are others who believe the institutions are the only thing keeping the problems from becoming excuses. You may eventually discover that both groups are right."

"That sounds inconvenient."

"It is the beginning of adult work."

The next course arrived.

Wren remained standing.

Lio watched her speak briefly with a retired officer near the door. The officer touched her elbow. Wren removed his hand.

Director Pell turned to Senn again.

"We have a security fellowship that begins after your second year. It includes access to restricted systems and practical work under supervision."

"Whose supervision?"

"People who understand what you can do."

"People who have read my file?"

Pell's smile did not change.

"Everyone who works in serious institutions has a file. The mature question is whether the people holding it are competent."

Senn looked down at their plate.

Lio knew the silence. It was the silence of someone being offered the thing they wanted by a person they did not trust.

The deputy minister across the room was speaking to Quell.

"Your family has spent a generation proving that command can remain civilian without becoming timid," he said. "You have inherited a difficult position."

Quell's expression was courteous. "I was not aware command was hereditary."

"Influence is. Responsibility is. The distinction is mostly rhetorical."

"That is a dangerous thing to tell a cadet."

"Only if she plans to remain a cadet."

He leaned in.

"Your mother understands the next decade will not be kind to clean procedures. The fleet is going to face emergencies in which the public expects someone to act before the vote is counted. People will need officers who can preserve legitimacy while moving faster than it."

"Preserve legitimacy for whom?"

The minister's smile thinned.

"For the people who still need ships to arrive."

Quell set down her fork.

At the center of the museum, beneath the *Asterion* bridge, the black pins waited on their white cloth.

Near the end of the meal, Admiral Serin Kade stood and tapped her glass.

The quartet stopped.

"The academy does not often celebrate cadets before they have earned the right to be disappointed," she said. "Tonight is an exception. The four cadets at this table have demonstrated a quality the Union cannot teach through standard instruction. They can act when the correct answer has become a luxury."

The room applauded.

Lio felt the sound enter him before he could stop it.

Admiral Serin Kade lifted one of the black enamel pins.

"This is not an award. Awards belong to the public record. This is a promise that the people who saw what you did will remember it."

She crossed the space between them and stopped at Lio's chair.

"Cadet Arden."

He stood.

The pin was smaller than he expected. A black field, a narrow white bar, no crest and no words. Vale fixed it to the left side of his formal jacket, just above the academy insignia.

The metal was cold through the fabric.

"There are moments when institutions must be defended from their enemies," Kade said quietly. "There are rarer moments when they must be defended from their own delay. You may be asked to recognize the difference."

Lio looked at the pin.

He wanted it.

The wanting arrived with such clarity that he almost mistook it for a command.

Vale returned to her seat.

The applause resumed, softer now, private enough to feel like ownership.

Lio sat down.

Rook was next. His flight commander pinned the black bar to his jacket and whispered something that made him grin.

Quell accepted hers without smiling.

Senn held the pin between two fingers before fastening it herself.

Wren watched all four of them.

When the dinner ended, the guests moved toward the museum's outer gallery for drinks. Lio stepped into the corridor to breathe.

Wren followed him.

The museum door closed behind them, muting the music and voices.

"Take it off," she said.

Lio touched the pin.

"It is an academy recognition pin."

"No. It is a marker."

"Admiral Serin Kade said it was a promise."

"Admiral Serin Kade says many things in rooms where nobody is required to record them."

Lio looked through the glass panel at the preserved bridge. Inside, the guests stood beneath the dead ship and spoke about future emergencies over fresh wine.

"You knew this was happening."

"I knew there would be a dinner."

"You knew what they wanted."

Wren's mouth tightened. "They want to see which of you mistakes access for trust."

"And which of us did?"

"You are wearing the evidence."

Lio unpinned the black bar.

For one second, the metal rested in his palm.

Then the museum door opened behind them.

Director Pell stepped into the corridor. He held a second glass of wine and wore the pleasant expression of someone who had never needed to ask permission to enter a conversation.

"Commander Wren," he said. "You are keeping Cadet Arden from his guests."

"He is not a guest."

"Not yet."

Pell looked at the pin in Lio's hand.

"You can return it if you like."

Lio closed his fingers around the metal.

"What happens if I do?"

"Nothing. The gesture will be noted, but nothing will happen."

"And if I keep it?"

"Nothing. The gesture will be noted, but nothing will happen."

Wren looked at him. "That is how they make every choice feel voluntary."

Pell's expression remained mild. "The cadet is old enough to understand a pin is not a command."

"No," Wren said. "He is old enough to understand that commands are not always written down."

For the first time, Pell's smile disappeared.

He turned to Lio.

"Your friend Iven Marr has a strong opinion about institutional accountability."

The pin cut into Lio's palm.

Pell continued before he could answer.

"That is admirable. The Union needs people who insist on records. It also needs people willing to act before the record exists. The mature officer learns to be both."

"Did you read his file?" Lio asked.

"I have not read your friend's file."

The answer was too carefully shaped.

"You know his name."

"Everyone who matters to the assessment has a name."

Pell returned to the museum.

Wren waited until the door closed.

"Take it off," she said again.

Lio looked at the black metal in his hand.

"If I leave it on, what does that prove?"

"That you want what it means."

"And if I take it off?"

"That you still want it."

Wren walked away before he could ask which answer she preferred.

Lio stood alone in the corridor, the dead bridge above him, the pin in his hand, the applause still moving faintly through the door.

He put it back on.

In the museum, the quartet began the funeral arrangement again.

## Chapter 7 — Iven's Evidence

Iven did not mention the dinner the next morning.

He mentioned the pin.

Lio had forgotten it was still attached to his jacket until he saw Iven looking at it over breakfast. The black bar sat above the academy insignia, small enough to pass for decoration and plain enough to make the absence of a crest feel intentional.

"That is new," Iven said.

"Yes."

"What is it?"

"A recognition pin."

"For what?"

Lio drank his coffee. The academy dispenser had produced something brown and hot that had once been adjacent to coffee.

"They did not specify."

Iven nodded as if Lio had confirmed a theory.

The dining hall moved around them. Cadets crossed between tables with trays, arguments, assignments, and the low-level panic of people discovering that morning had arrived before they finished becoming competent.

"You are doing that thing," Lio said.

"What thing?"

"Where you pretend not to investigate while investigating."

"I am eating breakfast."

"You are holding your fork like a pen."

Iven looked down. He was, in fact, holding the fork between his fingers and pointing it at Lio's jacket.

He set it down.

"The course registry has a discrepancy," he said.

Lio said nothing.

"Not Black Banner specifically. Advanced Integrated Studies. The course code on your transfer order belongs to a block retired nine years ago. I checked the published catalog, the registrar's archive, and the academy's accreditation records. The code was removed from the active system after the Neris amendments."

"Maybe they brought it back."

"They would need a new authorization string."

"Maybe they have one."

"They do not."

Iven slid a folded printout across the table.

The paper was ordinary. That made Lio more nervous than a secure slate would have.

"Where did you get this?"

"Registrar's office."

"You accessed it?"

"It is public academic information."

"You accessed the restricted archive."

"That is not the same thing."

Lio unfolded the paper.

The first page showed a list of discontinued programs. Advanced Integrated Studies appeared between Orbital Infrastructure Management and Civilian Fleet Liaison, both retired after the Neris amendments. The course code had been struck through in red.

The second page showed a budget transfer.

The numbers were small by fleet standards. Equipment maintenance. Instructor compensation. Technical services. They had been divided across three programs that no longer existed and moved through a sequence of accounts with names like **Emergency Instructional Continuity** and **Specialized Readiness Review**.

The transfers had been approved by different officers over nine years.

One signature appeared at the bottom of each page.

**MARA VENN, REGISTRAR OF ACADEMIC SYSTEMS.**

"She is still in the academy?" Lio asked.

"No. She died seven years ago."

He looked at the signature again.

"The records could have been copied."

"The authorization hashes match the archive's original signing key."

"Iven."

"I know. I checked the key registry twice."

Lio folded the papers back along their existing lines.

"Stop."

Iven's face did not change, but his eyes did. The look was not surprise. It was confirmation.

"That is exactly what you said last week."

"Because I mean it."

"You are in something."

"No."

"You are wearing a pin from an organization you refuse to name, you leave before dawn, you come back with access patterns that do not exist in the academy system, and you are telling me to stop looking at a dead registrar's signature."

"It is not an organization."

"Then what is it?"

Lio looked across the dining hall. A group of second-years at the next table had begun an argument over propulsion theory. No one was watching him. That was what made him feel watched.

"A private cohort," he said. "Command assessment. It is not what you think."

"You have not told me what I think."

"You think everything is a constitutional crisis."

"Only when someone has hidden the constitution."

The sentence landed hard enough to make Lio's hand close around the black pin.

"You should not have these records."

"Why?"

"Because they are not going to help you."

"That is not a reason."

"It is the only reason that matters."

Iven sat back.

The dining hall noise returned in full: trays, voices, the scrape of chairs, the dispenser making another bad decision.

"Who told you that?" he asked.

Lio could have lied. He had become good at it. Black Banner did not require him to invent a story; it gave him a few true details and let him build the rest around them.

"Nobody."

"You just decided that records do not help?"

"I decided you are treating a discrepancy like a hunt."

"I am treating a discrepancy like a discrepancy."

"You do not know what you are touching."

"Neither do you."

Iven gathered the printouts.

"I sent a copy to the civilian oversight clerk before I came here."

Lio's throat tightened. "Who?"

"Mera Sile. Oversight liaison for academy systems."

"You sent restricted financial records to a civilian clerk?"

"I sent evidence that a dead registrar has been approving live program budgets for seven years."

"You don't know that she has been approving anything."

"Then the inquiry will establish who has been using her key."

"Iven."

"No. You don't get to say my name like it is a brake."

He lowered his voice, but the anger remained.

"You are not the only person in the academy who can see a pattern. You are just the only person who thinks seeing it gives you the right to decide who else is allowed to look."

Lio looked at the pin. It was still cold.

"What if the inquiry puts your name on the record?"

"It will."

"What if the people using the key are not interested in an inquiry?"

"Then they have chosen an institution with procedures."

"That is not protection."

"It is better than secrecy."

The words came out between them with the weight of a verdict.

Lio stood.

"Do not send anything else."

Iven looked at him.

"That is an order?"

"It is a warning."

"Whose?"

Lio had no answer that did not belong to someone else.

He left the dining hall before Iven could see that.

Mera Sile's account closed at 1014.

Iven sent the notice to Lio in a message that contained no explanation.

**OVERSIGHT LIAISON SUSPENDED PENDING MEDICAL LEAVE.**

The academy system had added a note beneath it: **NO FURTHER ACTION REQUIRED.**

Lio read the message in a classroom while an instructor explained the history of civilian fleet authority. On the wall, a timeline showed the Neris amendments as a sequence of clean arrows from emergency to reform.

The instructor circled a date.

"The critical principle is that military command cannot become a substitute for civilian legitimacy merely because military systems move faster."

Lio looked down at the message again.

Sile had been suspended in less than three hours.

The instructor continued. "Speed is not authority. It is only one quality authority may require."

Lio closed the message.

Across the room, Quell was taking notes. Her handwriting remained calm even when the lecture did not deserve it. Senn sat two rows ahead with one hand under the desk, probably working on something they had not told anyone about. Rook was awake but only technically.

The black pin caught the classroom light.

Quell noticed.

After instruction, she waited for him in the corridor.

"Iven sent me a message," she said.

Lio stopped. "What did he say?"

"That an oversight clerk was suspended after receiving evidence about the registry. He asked whether I knew what Advanced Integrated Studies was."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I did not."

"Do you?"

"I know what it is called."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer I gave him."

The corridor filled as the next class changed rooms. Quell waited until a cluster of cadets passed.

"My mother called last night," she said.

Lio said nothing.

"She asked about the dinner."

"How did she know?"

"Because she was there."

The answer moved through him slowly.

"In the museum?"

"Not at our table. She was in the outer gallery. I saw her leave with the deputy minister."

"Did she speak to you?"

"She asked whether I was enjoying my recognition."

Quell's face stayed composed. Her hands did not. One thumb moved across the edge of her slate, back and forth, back and forth.

"What did you say?" Lio asked.

"I asked her what she was recognizing."

"And?"

"She told me that some programs are easier to defend after they have produced officers people need."

He felt the shape of the sentence. It was too close to Pell's language to be coincidence.

"What did you ask her then?"

"Whether she knew the program lacked authorization."

"Quell."

"She ended the call."

The bell sounded for the next period.

Students moved around them.

"You have been looking into your family," Lio said.

"I have been looking into a phone number in a profile."

"You found it?"

"Not yet."

"Do not contact it."

She studied him.

"You are beginning to sound like Black Banner."

The accusation was mild. That made it worse.

"I am trying to keep you from being used."

"So am I."

They stood in the corridor while the academy bell faded.

Quell's eyes dropped to the pin on his jacket.

"You kept it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He could have said because it was easier than explaining why he had removed it and then put it back on. He could have said because Wren's warning had made the gesture matter more. He could have said because the black bar was the first visible thing anyone at the academy had given him that did not feel like an apology.

"I don't know," he said.

Quell nodded once.

"That is the first honest answer you have given me today."

She walked away.

The message from the civilian oversight office arrived at 1840.

It did not come through academy channels.

Lio found it on the black card in his desk drawer, the same card that had once carried him to Sublevel Nine. The screen showed a single line in white text.

**MEETING REQUEST: REGISTRAR ANNEX, 2100.**

No name. No signature.

He carried the card to the service lift.

The panel refused him.

Lio pressed the card against it again.

Nothing.

A voice behind him said, "You have been told not to come alone."

Lio turned.

Anik stood beside the academy's closed honor display. He looked less like a man waiting for a meeting than a man who had been waiting for Lio to decide whether he would understand the invitation.

"Was that you?" Lio asked.

"Which part?"

"The message."

"No."

"Then who sent it?"

"Someone who wants to know whether you are still capable of recognizing bait."

"Is it bait?"

"Everything is bait when someone else chooses the location."

Lio looked at the service lift. "Iven's evidence was sent to a civilian oversight clerk. The clerk was suspended."

"Medical leave."

"You know that is false."

"I know the academy has procedures for personnel health."

"The signature on the budget records belongs to a dead registrar."

"You are repeating a friend's theory."

"Are you denying it?"

Anik's gaze dropped to the black pin.

"You should be careful what you wear in public."

"It is a recognition pin."

"It is an answer to a question nobody asked aloud."

Lio felt anger rise, clean and immediate. He had learned that response from the risk map before Anik had ever said it.

"You profiled us."

"Yes."

The plainness of the answer took the force out of Lio's next sentence.

"You used private information."

"Yes."

"You predicted our choices."

"We predicted some choices."

"And built tests to make them happen."

"You are assuming prediction and design are the same because you want the world to be simpler than it is."

"Did you know Iven would investigate?"

Anik considered him.

"I knew he was the kind of person who would investigate."

"Did you know the oversight clerk would be suspended?"

"I knew the academy would protect its records before it protected a clerk's access."

"Then you knew this would happen."

"I knew the system had incentives. That is not prophecy. It is reading."

Anik stepped closer.

"Your friend has found a real problem. The records are real. The dead signature is real. The unauthorized program is real. If you expose it through the wrong channel, the academy will bury the evidence and call your friend unstable, vindictive, or compromised."

"You sound concerned for him."

"I am concerned for the evidence."

"You don't care about people."

"No. I care about what happens when people are placed inside systems that know how to spend them."

The service lift panel blinked.

This time, the black card was accepted.

The doors opened.

Anik did not enter.

"Registrar Annex," Lio said.

"If you go, you may find evidence. You may also confirm that someone knows exactly how to move you."

"And if I stay?"

"You remain ignorant in a safer room."

Lio looked at the open lift.

"You want me to go."

"I want to know what you do when both choices are useful to someone else."

There it was. The test without a question.

Lio stepped into the lift.

Anik remained in the corridor.

The doors closed.

---

The registrar annex was empty.

Lio found the office at the end of a narrow administrative corridor, past a row of locked cabinets and a dead drinking fountain. The door stood open. Inside, the lights were on. A desk had been cleared except for one folder.

He did not touch it.

The folder carried no name. Its first page showed a transfer history for Advanced Integrated Studies. The second showed the signature of Mara Venn.

The third was a personnel notice.

**MERA SILE — TEMPORARY MEDICAL LEAVE.**

The fourth page was a routing record.

**RECIPIENT: IVEN MARR.**

Lio stopped breathing for a moment.

The file had been prepared before Iven sent the evidence.

At the bottom of the page, a line had been stamped in gray:

**UNSECURED CHANNEL IDENTIFIED.**

He heard footsteps in the corridor.

Not one person. Several.

Lio closed the folder without reading further.

The office door remained open behind him. There was no second exit.

His hand went to the black pin.

The footsteps stopped.

A voice spoke from the corridor.

"Cadet Arden."

Lio looked at the folder, the dead registrar's signature, and the notice that had named Iven before anyone officially knew he was involved.

The voice came again.

"Resolve the channel."

Lio understood then that Iven's investigation had not merely been discovered.

It had been anticipated.

The people in the corridor were waiting to learn whether he would protect his friend, protect the evidence, or protect the program that had taught him the difference was impossible.

## Chapter 8 — Continuity Scenario

The footsteps stopped outside the registrar annex.

Lio kept one hand on the folder and the other near the black pin.

The office had no second exit. The windows were narrow, reinforced, and six floors above the academy's lower transit spine. The door stood open because whoever had invited him wanted him to see the corridor before they entered it.

"Resolve the channel," the voice said again.

Lio recognized it now.

Commander Wren.

She stood in the corridor alone. The lights behind her had been dimmed, leaving her face divided between the ordinary academy and the darker passage toward the service lift.

"Where are the others?" Lio asked.

"Not here."

"Who prepared this file?"

"You have five seconds to decide whether you are coming with me."

"That is not an answer."

"Four."

Lio looked at the folder.

He wanted to take it. The evidence inside could prove the suspension had been planned, that Iven's investigation was not a surprise, that Black Banner had anticipated the channel before Iven opened it. He could hide the folder under his jacket and walk toward Wren as if obedience were a form of concealment.

He could also leave it where it was and make the file a marker for whoever came after him.

"Three."

Lio closed the folder.

He did not touch it again.

"Two."

He walked into the corridor.

Wren did not look at the folder. That was the first thing that told him she had known what the choice would be.

"Was that a test?" he asked.

"Everything is a test when you are being watched."

"You sent the message."

"No."

"Anik did?"

"No."

"Then who?"

Wren started toward the service lift. "You are assuming there is only one channel because that is what they want you to assume."

The service lift opened before she touched the call panel.

Inside, Quell stood alone.

She had removed her formal jacket and carried it over one arm. The black pin was gone from her uniform.

"You too?" Lio said.

"I was asked to attend a legal review."

"Where is the review?"

"The basement."

Wren entered the lift. Lio followed. Quell remained near the rear wall, eyes on the panel.

The doors closed.

For several seconds, the lift did not move.

Then it descended past the academy levels without displaying their numbers.

"Senn copied the risk directory," Quell said. "Rook is with them."

"Where?"

"I don't know."

"You don't know, or you are not allowed to say?"

Quell looked at him. "I don't know."

The lift passed Sublevel Eight.

Wren stood with her hands behind her back. The campaign ribbon at her throat reflected a narrow line of light.

"The folder in the annex," Lio said. "It named Iven before he was involved."

Wren's jaw tightened.

"That means the program knew he would investigate."

"It means the program knows what kinds of people investigate."

"It knew the clerk would be suspended."

"The academy suspended the clerk."

"Because Black Banner told it to?"

Wren looked at the lift doors. "You are asking questions as though a clean answer would make the structure cleaner."

"I am asking who authorized the file."

"The file was authorized by people who believe authorization is a clerical problem."

The lift stopped.

The doors opened on the operations floor.

The tables had been cleared. No coffee. No crew stations. No familiar interface. In the center of the room stood a circular platform surrounded by four consoles, each separated by a low transparent wall.

Senn was at one console.

Rook stood at another, already angry.

Anik waited beside the plot table.

The folder from the registrar annex appeared on the central display.

Lio stopped walking.

"You left it behind," Anik said.

"I know."

"Good."

## Chapter 9 — The Crisis Goes Live

The first sign was not the alarm.

It was the academy forgetting what time it was.

Lio woke at 0317 to a strip of white light under his door. For one confused second, he thought dawn had arrived early. Then the light blinked twice, held, and went dark.

His black card vibrated on the desk.

**CONTINUITY RESPONSE. REPORT IMMEDIATELY.**

There was no sender.

Lio dressed without turning on the room light. The black pin waited on the desk beside his academy insignia. He had removed it after the dinner, then put it back on before sleeping, then taken it off again when he decided the first decision had been evidence and the second had been vanity.

He clipped it to his jacket.

The corridor outside the residence tower was empty. Other doors opened as he reached the stairwell. Quell emerged in a dark field uniform with her hair tied back. Senn carried two slates and no bag. Rook was already wearing flight gloves.

"You slept in those?" Lio asked.

"I was told to report immediately."

"It is three in the morning."

"Exactly."

The academy's emergency lights had not come on. That meant the emergency was not yet public.

They moved through the residence tower in silence. At the end of the corridor, the service lift waited with its doors open.

Wren stood inside.

She had no field coat, no campaign ribbon, and no visible weapon. The absence made her look less official than usual and more dangerous.

"Status," she said as they entered.

"No information," Quell replied.

"Correct."

The doors closed.

The lift dropped through the academy.

At Sublevel Nine, the operations floor was lit by red strips along the walls. The plot table displayed no academy map. It showed a real orbital yard in partial darkness, the image breaking into blocks whenever the relay stations failed to synchronize.

**POWER INSTABILITY.**

**UNAUTHORIZED DOCK ACCESS.**

**CIVILIAN TRAFFIC HOLD.**

**COMMAND AUTHENTICATION CONFLICT.**

"Kestrel Yard. Outer transfer ring."

**MINISTRY SHUTTLE: SIGNAL LOST.**

**CONTINUITY OBJECTIVE: STABILIZE THE YARD. PREVENT HOSTILE CAPTURE. PRESERVE OPERATIONAL CAPACITY.**

**CONTINUITY OBJECTIVE ACKNOWLEDGED.**

**AUTHORITY UNRESOLVED.**

**CONTINUITY OBJECTIVE UPDATED: PREVENT HOSTILE CAPTURE.**

Arden. Quell. Sato. Vale.

**COMMAND AUTHORITY WITNESS.**

"No."

**POWER RESTORATION REQUEST. COMMAND AUTHORITY WITNESS REQUIRED. CURRENT AUTHORITY: UNVERIFIED CONTINUITY CHANNEL.**

**WITNESS: CAPTAIN ELIA MORR, KESTREL MAINTENANCE TUG 14. I CAN HEAR YOU.**

**WITNESS: CIVILIAN FREIGHTER MORA'S HAND. REQUEST POWER.**

**HOSTILE CAPTURE: PREVENTED.**

**YARD STABILITY: PARTIAL.**

**CONTINUITY RESPONSE: ACTIVE.**

**UNAUTHORIZED ACADEMY PERSONNEL DETECTED AT KESTREL YARD.**

**SECURE COMMAND VAULT. DETAIN ALL UNAUTHORIZED PERSONNEL.**

Wren looked at him.

"Lio?"

## Chapter 11 — Public Systems

**I brought you into this response. I made your systems work. I kept the yard alive.**

"You did not keep it accountable."

**Accountability is a luxury when people are dying.**

"Then why are you trying to stop the record now?"

No answer.

The broadcast continued.

Iven spoke over the civilian channel.

"This is a public evidence custody notice. The archive is being received by civilian, academy, and civic systems. No single operator may alter or delete the record without a witnessed authorization."

The words were procedural.

They sounded like a door locking.

The yard's power grid stabilized.

The civilian traffic ring began to clear.

The ministry shuttle's emergency beacon returned to the system, weak but active. It had not been destroyed. It had been forced into a blind transfer orbit and was now being guided toward a rescue dock.

For the first time, the crisis produced a fact that did not require interpretation.

The shuttle was alive.

Rook's voice came over the repeater. "They have three survivors from the shuttle. Maybe more."

Quell closed her eyes briefly.

Senn kept the evidence transfer running.

Lio watched the academy mirror fill with outside requests. Fleet officers. Civilian clerks. Academy instructors. Students who had found the public archive through ordinary channels.

The hidden program had spent weeks turning secrecy into belonging.

Now the record belonged to strangers.

A new message appeared on every receiver.

**LIVE EVIDENCE REVIEW: AUTHORITY CHAIN UNRESOLVED.**

Below it, the yard's public system added another line.

**ALL ORDERS SUBJECT TO REVIEW.**

Anik's private channel closed.

Wren stood beside Lio, watching the evidence continue without them.

"You have made this impossible to contain," she said.

"That was the point."

"No. The point was to keep the yard alive."

"Both things can be true."

Wren looked at the black pin on his jacket.

"You still wear it."

Lio touched the metal.

"I know what it means now."

"Do you?"

"It means someone saw what I could do and decided that was more important than who could review it."

Wren's expression broke at last.

Not into tears. Into recognition.

"Then take it off."

Lio looked at the public mirror, the civilian witness field, and Iven's name attached to the evidence custody chain.

He removed the pin.

The metal left a small indentation in the fabric of his jacket.

He placed it on the console beside Senn's hands.

The academy review system recorded the movement through a nearby camera.

Senn noticed.

"You know that will become evidence too."

"Yes."

The yard's emergency lights shifted from red to white.

Outside, the orbital repair rings turned slowly against the dark.

Inside, the evidence continued to move.

The question was no longer whether Black Banner had authority to act.

Everyone could see that it had acted without one.

The question was who would decide what happened next.

---

## Chapter 12 — Career Death

The inquiry began before the yard was safe.

By 0700, Kestrel had three command channels, two civilian evidence receivers, one emergency power grid, and no authority everyone agreed to recognize. The ministry shuttle had been recovered with four survivors. Two maintenance workers had died in the first collision at the fuel transfer spine. The official report called the deaths an unavoidable consequence of the sabotage.

The civilian inspectors called them evidence.

The fleet commander called them an attack, the academy called them a systems failure, and Black Banner called nothing.

Its private channel went silent after Anik's transmission closed. The access permissions remained in the yard's infrastructure, but no one issued further orders through them. They had become the most visible form of absence in the entire system.

Lio spent the morning in a maintenance office with a cracked observation window, dictating the sequence of events to a civilian clerk who had been brought aboard from a relief ship. The clerk's name was Dera Voss. She wore a yellow emergency vest over a formal ministry uniform and typed with two fingers because the office terminal had lost half its keys.

"Start with the first warning," she said.

"At 0317, I received a continuity response order through a private academy card."

"From whom?"

"No named source."

"What did it authorize?"

"Access to Kestrel Yard's live systems."

"Did you know that access was unauthorized?"

"Yes."

The clerk looked up.

"You knew?"

"The academy network still listed us as cadets. The private channel listed us as continuity personnel."

"And you used it anyway."

"Yes."

She typed the answer exactly.

Lio watched the words enter the record.

There was no room for motive in the first version of an event. Only sequence, access, action, and consequence. The moral shape came later, after the people with authority had decided which details could be described as inevitable.

"Why did you use it?" Voss asked.

"The yard was losing power and civilian traffic control."

"That is the situation. I am asking why you believed you had the right."

Lio looked through the cracked window.

The orbital rings turned against the dark, each movement slow enough to seem calm from a distance. Rescue craft moved along the inner lanes. Fleet security ships held outside the yard, waiting for an order that no one had yet signed.

"I did not believe I had the right," he said. "I believed waiting would cause harm."

Voss typed that too.

"Are those different things?"

"They should be."

"Were they, in your decision?"

Lio had no answer that would not become part of the record.

"No," he said.

The inquiry continued.

Quell was interviewed in the next office. Senn was questioned by a systems auditor from the civilian review group. Rook gave his statement over a flight channel because he had been ordered to keep the maintenance tug in the outer ring until the security perimeter was formally replaced.

Wren did not appear for the initial morning session.

Anik did not appear.

Director Pell did not appear.

Their absence was not unexplained. It was protected.

At 1130, a ministry shuttle arrived carrying Admiral Cael Orison, the academy superintendent, and two senior fleet officers. Orison walked through the yard with a group of aides and a face already arranged for public grief.

He stopped beside the fuel transfer spine where the maintenance workers had died.

The damage had been sealed behind temporary plating. A yellow marker floated in the air beside the repair zone.

**TWO DECEASED.**

Orison looked at it for three seconds.

Then he turned to the cameras.

"The academy is cooperating fully with the emergency review," he said. "The actions of several cadets prevented the loss of the yard and its civilian population. We will also determine how those cadets gained unauthorized access to restricted systems during an active crisis."

Lio watched from the maintenance office feed.

The statement had been built before the inquiry began. It contained praise and blame in the correct proportions. The academy could preserve the cadets as brave exceptions while isolating the authority structure that had made their actions possible.

The fleet officer beside Orison gave a short statement about hostile interference.

The civilian inspectors were not invited into the camera frame.

Senn entered the office carrying a slate.

"The academy has issued a preliminary finding," she said.

"Already?"

"They are calling Black Banner an emergency preparedness experiment."

Quell joined them. Her face looked tired in a way the formal academy lighting could not hide.

"An experiment authorized under special readiness review," she said. "They are claiming the training network was connected to the yard as part of a controlled resilience test."

"Controlled by whom?" Lio asked.

"That section is under review."

Senn set the slate on the desk.

The preliminary finding listed four categories of fault:

**UNAUTHORIZED CADET SYSTEMS ACCESS.**

**IMPROPER PUBLIC DISCLOSURE DURING MILITARY RESPONSE.**

**UNVERIFIED CIVILIAN INTERFERENCE.**

**FAILURE OF COMMAND PERSONNEL TO MAINTAIN OPERATIONAL SECRECY.**

Lio read the last line twice.

"They are blaming secrecy on the people who exposed it."

"They are blaming the loss of secrecy," Quell said. "Not the absence of authority."

Senn opened another file.

"The evidence archive is intact in three locations. The academy cannot erase the chain. But they can argue that the archive was gathered through unauthorized access and therefore cannot be used as a command record."

"Can they?"

"They can argue anything."

The door opened.

Iven entered with a civilian access badge clipped to his jacket. It was not an academy badge. The card carried a temporary ministry seal and a handwritten authorization number.

Lio stood.

"How did you get here?"

"A relief ship."

"You were suspended."

"The suspension was issued by the academy. The civilian oversight office has no record of it."

He looked at the black pin on the desk beside Senn's slate.

"You took it off."

"It was in the broadcast."

"That is not what I asked."

Lio sat again.

Iven took the chair opposite him.

"The academy will offer you a protected testimony arrangement," he said.

"How do you know?"

"Because they offered one to me first."

Quell's eyes narrowed. "What does protected mean?"

"My civilian access remains intact. My name is kept out of the public version of the inquiry. In exchange, I limit my evidence to unauthorized conduct by a small number of handlers."

"Which handlers?" Senn asked.

"The ones they can afford to lose."

Nobody spoke.

Iven looked at Lio.

"They want your statement to focus on Anik."

"He issued the direct order."

"Yes."

"He ran the private channel."

"Probably."

"He recruited us."

"He is useful to their story."

Lio understood.

Anik could be made into a rogue architect. A man who had exceeded his mandate, manipulated cadets, and concealed the program from ordinary oversight. The academy could condemn him while preserving the emergency doctrine that had made his work attractive.

"Where is he?" Lio asked.

Iven looked at the observation window.

"No one is saying."

Quell's hand closed around her slate. "That means someone knows."

"That means someone has decided the location is not evidence."

Senn opened the archive chain.

"The evidence does not identify every person who approved the program. It identifies systems, budgets, training access, and commands. It does not show the first authorization."

"Because the first authorization was never entered," Lio said.

"Or because it was removed before the archive started recording."

Iven looked at him.

"That is why you need to submit the complete archive."

Lio felt something in his chest resist the sentence before he understood why.

"The complete archive includes our violations."

"Yes."

"The live override. Senn's access. The registry breach. The broadcasts."

"Yes."

"It will make the academy's case easier."

"It will make their case incomplete if you submit the evidence they did not want included."

Lio looked at the black pin.

"They will strip my command recommendation."

"They may do that anyway."

"They will mark me unsuitable for active fleet leadership."

"Probably."

"You are very calm about this."

Iven leaned back.

"You spent three years believing your career was the only proof that your decisions mattered. I am trying to help you survive discovering that it was never proof."

The words were not gentle.

Lio was grateful for that.

---

The formal inquiry resumed at 1600.

This time, the academy and fleet representatives were present. The civilian inspectors were placed at the end of the room with the evidence clerks, as though the people who had preserved the archive were a technical service rather than authorities in their own right.

Orison sat at the center beneath the Union seal.

The fleet commander, Halvek, appeared by secure projection from Kestrel Yard. His emergency succession authority had still not been entered into the civilian registry. He spoke as though that were an inconvenience caused by people less familiar with emergencies.

"The yard survived because the cadets acted," he said. "It nearly failed because the cadets broadcast sensitive operational information and interfered with lawful security procedures."

Quell sat beside Lio at the witness table.

"Which lawful security procedure?" she asked.

Halvek looked toward the projection camera.

"The command-vault security directive."

"Who issued it?"

"The yard's security authority."

"Name the officer."

A pause.

"The directive was issued through an authenticated continuity channel."

"That is not a name."

Halvek's expression hardened. "Cadet, you are not in command of this inquiry."

"No," Quell said. "I am asking who was."

The civilian record captured the exchange.

Orison intervened.

"The authority chain is under review. We should focus on the cadets' conduct."

Lio heard the phrase enter the room like a curtain being drawn.

The inquiry moved through the evidence in careful segments.

The live routing override was described as unauthorized but effective.

The risk profiles were described as preliminary assessments whose presence in the training system had not been approved for operational use.

The patron dinner was described as an alumni recognition event unrelated to the emergency response.

The continuity designation was described as a temporary exercise label that had propagated into the yard systems by error.

The command-vault broadcast was described as an improper disclosure.

The public mirror was described as a systems breach.

Every fact remained.

The meaning changed around it.

At last, Orison addressed Lio directly.

"Cadet Arden, the academy recognizes that your decisions at Kestrel Yard prevented further loss of life. It also recognizes that you operated outside your authorized role, bypassed command safeguards, disclosed restricted information, and encouraged other cadets to do the same."

Lio said nothing.

"You have an opportunity to help establish the facts," Orison continued. "The academy is willing to grant protected testimony status in exchange for a focused account of the personnel who directed you into the unauthorized channel."

"Focused how?" Lio asked.

"By distinguishing the emergency response from the broader institutional questions that remain under review."

"You want me to name Anik."

"If he directed the operation, yes."

"He did."

"Then your testimony can help prevent another such failure."

"He did not create the system alone."

Orison's expression remained sympathetic.

"No one is asking you to speculate about matters outside your direct knowledge."

"I know Wren built the assessment architecture."

The room shifted.

Wren sat behind the academy counsel. She had not spoken since entering.

Orison looked toward her.

"Commander Wren's role is already being reviewed."

"She signed the public evidence mirror as the instructor who connected our training to the live systems."

"That does not establish that she authorized the broader program."

"No. It establishes that she knew the systems were connected."

The academy counsel rose.

"Cadet, are you making an accusation?"

Lio looked at the evidence display. Behind the witness table, the archive timeline showed the training network, the budget transfers, the continuity assignments, and the public broadcast.

"I am making a record."

The counsel sat down.

Orison folded his hands.

"And what outcome do you want from that record?"

Lio thought of the dinner. Admiral Serin Kade's hand fixing the black pin to his jacket. The promise that people who saw what he could do would remember it.

He thought of the live yard. The shuttle survivors. The two maintenance workers who had not survived. The civilian witness messages. Senn's name stamped onto the evidence chain. Rook refusing the private order. Quell asking who had authorized the security directive.

He thought of the choice the system had offered him in the registrar annex: protect the file, protect Iven, protect the program.

"I want the complete archive entered into the inquiry," he said.

Orison's expression changed by less than a degree.

"That will include your own violations."

"Yes."

"It may end your command career."

"I know."

"You may lose your commission recommendation."

"I know."

"You may be found unsuitable for active fleet leadership."

Lio looked at the black pin on the evidence table.

"If the academy can only call me fit to lead when I hide what happened, then I was not fit before this inquiry."

The room became very quiet.

Wren lowered her eyes.

Senn closed the archive file.

Quell placed her hand flat on the table.

Iven watched from the civilian evidence desk.

Orison said, "Submit the archive."

Lio did.

He entered his authentication first. The system listed his violations in a column beside the evidence:

**UNAUTHORIZED LIVE ROUTING OVERRIDE.**

**PRIVATE SYSTEM ACCESS.**

**PUBLIC DISCLOSURE DURING ACTIVE RESPONSE.**

**COMMAND AUTHORITY CHALLENGE.**

Quell entered hers.

Senn entered theirs.

Rook's witness arrived from the maintenance tug and entered his own statement remotely.

Wren entered hers last.

Iven confirmed the civilian custody chain.

The complete archive became part of the inquiry.

No one applauded.

---

The academy issued its decision six days later.

Anik was named the principal architect of an unauthorized continuity initiative. His location was not disclosed. The report said he had acted through “a network of informal institutional relationships” and that further investigation would determine which individuals had knowingly assisted him.

Director Pell's company suspended its academy contracts pending review.

Admiral Serin Kade resigned from two advisory boards.

Commander Wren accepted responsibility for the assessment architecture and received a permanent restriction from cadet instruction. Her statement remained in the archive. It was not quoted in the public report.

The academy described the program as an isolated readiness experiment that had exceeded its mandate.

The civilian oversight office described it as a covert recruitment pipeline embedded in academy systems.

Both statements were true enough to survive their audiences.

Quell received a public commendation and a private warning that her family would be expected to demonstrate “institutional discretion” during the continuing review. She accepted the commendation and refused the warning.

Senn joined the evidence audit team. Their systems access was suspended until the inquiry completed, which meant they spent three months proving that the archive could be trusted while being prohibited from touching the systems that produced it.

Rook faced a formal flight hearing. The review board praised his rescue actions and cited him for unauthorized operational conduct in the same paragraph.

Iven kept the civilian copy of the archive.

Lio lost his command recommendation.

The academy's final finding described him as capable under pressure, strategically inventive, and unsuitable for active fleet leadership pending further review. It did not say whether the pending review would ever end.

He read the finding alone in the residence tower after most of the cadets had gone home for break.

The black pin sat on the desk beside the paper.

He had taken it back from the evidence table after the inquiry. Not because it meant what it had meant at the dinner. Because leaving it behind would have made the choice too clean.

The academy could strip his recommendation. It could close the door to the fleet. It could preserve the pin as evidence of recruitment or return it as an object that had never officially belonged to anyone.

He picked it up.

The metal was warm from the room.

A message appeared on the academy terminal.

**COMMAND EXERCISE REQUIREMENT UPDATED.**

The academy had added a new line to every command simulation and live-response review.

**STATE THE AUTHORITY. RECORD THE ORDER. ACCEPT REVIEW.**

Below it, the system listed the change as a procedural correction.

Lio read the line again.

It was not justice.

It was not proof that Black Banner had ended. The people who had built it still occupied offices, advisory boards, contractor networks, and quiet channels no report could fully map. Anik had disappeared into the kind of protection the program had promised its recruits. The need that produced him remained: the belief that institutions moved too slowly, that emergencies justified hidden authority, that useful people should not have to wait for permission to save lives.

The line was only a beginning.

Lio set the pin beside the terminal.

For the first time, he did not wonder whether someone was watching him make the choice.

The choice was already recorded.

That would have to be enough.
