# Black Banner Academy: Chapter 12 Draft

## Career Death

The inquiry began before the yard was safe.

By 0700, Kestrel had three command channels, two civilian evidence receivers, one emergency power grid, and no authority everyone agreed to recognize. The ministry shuttle had been recovered with four survivors. Two maintenance workers had died in the first collision at the fuel transfer spine. The official report called the deaths an unavoidable consequence of the sabotage.

The civilian inspectors called them evidence.

The fleet commander called them an attack, the academy called them a systems failure, and Black Banner called nothing.

Its private channel went silent after Anik's transmission closed. The access permissions remained in the yard's infrastructure, but no one issued further orders through them. They had become the most visible form of absence in the entire system.

Lio spent the morning in a maintenance office with a cracked observation window, dictating the sequence of events to a civilian clerk who had been brought aboard from a relief ship. The clerk's name was Dera Voss. She wore a yellow emergency vest over a formal ministry uniform and typed with two fingers because the office terminal had lost half its keys.

"Start with the first warning," she said.

"At 0317, I received a continuity response order through a private academy card."

"From whom?"

"No named source."

"What did it authorize?"

"Access to Kestrel Yard's live systems."

"Did you know that access was unauthorized?"

"Yes."

The clerk looked up.

"You knew?"

"The academy network still listed us as cadets. The private channel listed us as continuity personnel."

"And you used it anyway."

"Yes."

She typed the answer exactly.

Lio watched the words enter the record.

There was no room for motive in the first version of an event. Only sequence, access, action, and consequence. The moral shape came later, after the people with authority had decided which details could be described as inevitable.

"Why did you use it?" Voss asked.

"The yard was losing power and civilian traffic control."

"That is the situation. I am asking why you believed you had the right."

Lio looked through the cracked window.

The orbital rings turned against the dark, each movement slow enough to seem calm from a distance. Rescue craft moved along the inner lanes. Fleet security ships held outside the yard, waiting for an order that no one had yet signed.

"I did not believe I had the right," he said. "I believed waiting would cause harm."

Voss typed that too.

"Are those different things?"

"They should be."

"Were they, in your decision?"

Lio had no answer that would not become part of the record.

"No," he said.

The inquiry continued.

Quell was interviewed in the next office. Senn was questioned by a systems auditor from the civilian review group. Rook gave his statement over a flight channel because he had been ordered to keep the maintenance tug in the outer ring until the security perimeter was formally replaced.

Wren did not appear for the initial morning session.

Anik did not appear.

Director Pell did not appear.

Their absence was not unexplained. It was protected.

At 1130, a ministry shuttle arrived carrying Admiral Cael Orison, the academy superintendent, and two senior fleet officers. Orison walked through the yard with a group of aides and a face already arranged for public grief.

He stopped beside the fuel transfer spine where the maintenance workers had died.

The damage had been sealed behind temporary plating. A yellow marker floated in the air beside the repair zone.

**TWO DECEASED.**

Orison looked at it for three seconds.

Then he turned to the cameras.

"The academy is cooperating fully with the emergency review," he said. "The actions of several cadets prevented the loss of the yard and its civilian population. We will also determine how those cadets gained unauthorized access to restricted systems during an active crisis."

Lio watched from the maintenance office feed.

The statement had been built before the inquiry began. It contained praise and blame in the correct proportions. The academy could preserve the cadets as brave exceptions while isolating the authority structure that had made their actions possible.

The fleet officer beside Orison gave a short statement about hostile interference.

The civilian inspectors were not invited into the camera frame.

Senn entered the office carrying a slate.

"The academy has issued a preliminary finding," she said.

"Already?"

"They are calling Black Banner an emergency preparedness experiment."

Quell joined them. Her face looked tired in a way the formal academy lighting could not hide.

"An experiment authorized under special readiness review," she said. "They are claiming the training network was connected to the yard as part of a controlled resilience test."

"Controlled by whom?" Lio asked.

"That section is under review."

Senn set the slate on the desk.

The preliminary finding listed four categories of fault:

**UNAUTHORIZED CADET SYSTEMS ACCESS.**

**IMPROPER PUBLIC DISCLOSURE DURING MILITARY RESPONSE.**

**UNVERIFIED CIVILIAN INTERFERENCE.**

**FAILURE OF COMMAND PERSONNEL TO MAINTAIN OPERATIONAL SECRECY.**

Lio read the last line twice.

"They are blaming secrecy on the people who exposed it."

"They are blaming the loss of secrecy," Quell said. "Not the absence of authority."

Senn opened another file.

"The evidence archive is intact in three locations. The academy cannot erase the chain. But they can argue that the archive was gathered through unauthorized access and therefore cannot be used as a command record."

"Can they?"

"They can argue anything."

The door opened.

Iven entered with a civilian access badge clipped to his jacket. It was not an academy badge. The card carried a temporary ministry seal and a handwritten authorization number.

Lio stood.

"How did you get here?"

"A relief ship."

"You were suspended."

"The suspension was issued by the academy. The civilian oversight office has no record of it."

He looked at the black pin on the desk beside Senn's slate.

"You took it off."

"It was in the broadcast."

"That is not what I asked."

Lio sat again.

Iven took the chair opposite him.

"The academy will offer you a protected testimony arrangement," he said.

"How do you know?"

"Because they offered one to me first."

Quell's eyes narrowed. "What does protected mean?"

"My civilian access remains intact. My name is kept out of the public version of the inquiry. In exchange, I limit my evidence to unauthorized conduct by a small number of handlers."

"Which handlers?" Senn asked.

"The ones they can afford to lose."

Nobody spoke.

Iven looked at Lio.

"They want your statement to focus on Anik."

"He issued the direct order."

"Yes."

"He ran the private channel."

"Probably."

"He recruited us."

"He is useful to their story."

Lio understood.

Anik could be made into a rogue architect. A man who had exceeded his mandate, manipulated cadets, and concealed the program from ordinary oversight. The academy could condemn him while preserving the emergency doctrine that had made his work attractive.

"Where is he?" Lio asked.

Iven looked at the observation window.

"No one is saying."

Quell's hand closed around her slate. "That means someone knows."

"That means someone has decided the location is not evidence."

Senn opened the archive chain.

"The evidence does not identify every person who approved the program. It identifies systems, budgets, training access, and commands. It does not show the first authorization."

"Because the first authorization was never entered," Lio said.

"Or because it was removed before the archive started recording."

Iven looked at him.

"That is why you need to submit the complete archive."

Lio felt something in his chest resist the sentence before he understood why.

"The complete archive includes our violations."

"Yes."

"The live override. Senn's access. The registry breach. The broadcasts."

"Yes."

"It will make the academy's case easier."

"It will make their case incomplete if you submit the evidence they did not want included."

Lio looked at the black pin.

"They will strip my command recommendation."

"They may do that anyway."

"They will mark me unsuitable for active fleet leadership."

"Probably."

"You are very calm about this."

Iven leaned back.

"You spent three years believing your career was the only proof that your decisions mattered. I am trying to help you survive discovering that it was never proof."

The words were not gentle.

Lio was grateful for that.

---

The formal inquiry resumed at 1600.

This time, the academy and fleet representatives were present. The civilian inspectors were placed at the end of the room with the evidence clerks, as though the people who had preserved the archive were a technical service rather than authorities in their own right.

Orison sat at the center beneath the Union seal.

The fleet commander, Halvek, appeared by secure projection from Kestrel Yard. His emergency succession authority had still not been entered into the civilian registry. He spoke as though that were an inconvenience caused by people less familiar with emergencies.

"The yard survived because the cadets acted," he said. "It nearly failed because the cadets broadcast sensitive operational information and interfered with lawful security procedures."

Quell sat beside Lio at the witness table.

"Which lawful security procedure?" she asked.

Halvek looked toward the projection camera.

"The command-vault security directive."

"Who issued it?"

"The yard's security authority."

"Name the officer."

A pause.

"The directive was issued through an authenticated continuity channel."

"That is not a name."

Halvek's expression hardened. "Cadet, you are not in command of this inquiry."

"No," Quell said. "I am asking who was."

The civilian record captured the exchange.

Orison intervened.

"The authority chain is under review. We should focus on the cadets' conduct."

Lio heard the phrase enter the room like a curtain being drawn.

The inquiry moved through the evidence in careful segments.

The live routing override was described as unauthorized but effective.

The risk profiles were described as preliminary assessments whose presence in the training system had not been approved for operational use.

The patron dinner was described as an alumni recognition event unrelated to the emergency response.

The continuity designation was described as a temporary exercise label that had propagated into the yard systems by error.

The command-vault broadcast was described as an improper disclosure.

The public mirror was described as a systems breach.

Every fact remained.

The meaning changed around it.

At last, Orison addressed Lio directly.

"Cadet Arden, the academy recognizes that your decisions at Kestrel Yard prevented further loss of life. It also recognizes that you operated outside your authorized role, bypassed command safeguards, disclosed restricted information, and encouraged other cadets to do the same."

Lio said nothing.

"You have an opportunity to help establish the facts," Orison continued. "The academy is willing to grant protected testimony status in exchange for a focused account of the personnel who directed you into the unauthorized channel."

"Focused how?" Lio asked.

"By distinguishing the emergency response from the broader institutional questions that remain under review."

"You want me to name Anik."

"If he directed the operation, yes."

"He did."

"Then your testimony can help prevent another such failure."

"He did not create the system alone."

Orison's expression remained sympathetic.

"No one is asking you to speculate about matters outside your direct knowledge."

"I know Wren built the assessment architecture."

The room shifted.

Wren sat behind the academy counsel. She had not spoken since entering.

Orison looked toward her.

"Commander Wren's role is already being reviewed."

"She signed the public evidence mirror as the instructor who connected our training to the live systems."

"That does not establish that she authorized the broader program."

"No. It establishes that she knew the systems were connected."

The academy counsel rose.

"Cadet, are you making an accusation?"

Lio looked at the evidence display. Behind the witness table, the archive timeline showed the training network, the budget transfers, the continuity assignments, and the public broadcast.

"I am making a record."

The counsel sat down.

Orison folded his hands.

"And what outcome do you want from that record?"

Lio thought of the dinner. Admiral Serin Kade's hand fixing the black pin to his jacket. The promise that people who saw what he could do would remember it.

He thought of the live yard. The shuttle survivors. The two maintenance workers who had not survived. The civilian witness messages. Senn's name stamped onto the evidence chain. Rook refusing the private order. Quell asking who had authorized the security directive.

He thought of the choice the system had offered him in the registrar annex: protect the file, protect Iven, protect the program.

"I want the complete archive entered into the inquiry," he said.

Orison's expression changed by less than a degree.

"That will include your own violations."

"Yes."

"It may end your command career."

"I know."

"You may lose your commission recommendation."

"I know."

"You may be found unsuitable for active fleet leadership."

Lio looked at the black pin on the evidence table.

"If the academy can only call me fit to lead when I hide what happened, then I was not fit before this inquiry."

The room became very quiet.

Wren lowered her eyes.

Senn closed the archive file.

Quell placed her hand flat on the table.

Iven watched from the civilian evidence desk.

Orison said, "Submit the archive."

Lio did.

He entered his authentication first. The system listed his violations in a column beside the evidence:

**UNAUTHORIZED LIVE ROUTING OVERRIDE.**

**PRIVATE SYSTEM ACCESS.**

**PUBLIC DISCLOSURE DURING ACTIVE RESPONSE.**

**COMMAND AUTHORITY CHALLENGE.**

Quell entered hers.

Senn entered theirs.

Rook's witness arrived from the maintenance tug and entered his own statement remotely.

Wren entered hers last.

Iven confirmed the civilian custody chain.

The complete archive became part of the inquiry.

No one applauded.

---

The academy issued its decision six days later.

Anik was named the principal architect of an unauthorized continuity initiative. His location was not disclosed. The report said he had acted through “a network of informal institutional relationships” and that further investigation would determine which individuals had knowingly assisted him.

Director Pell's company suspended its academy contracts pending review.

Admiral Serin Kade resigned from two advisory boards.

Commander Wren accepted responsibility for the assessment architecture and received a permanent restriction from cadet instruction. Her statement remained in the archive. It was not quoted in the public report.

The academy described the program as an isolated readiness experiment that had exceeded its mandate.

The civilian oversight office described it as a covert recruitment pipeline embedded in academy systems.

Both statements were true enough to survive their audiences.

Quell received a public commendation and a private warning that her family would be expected to demonstrate “institutional discretion” during the continuing review. She accepted the commendation and refused the warning.

Senn joined the evidence audit team. Their systems access was suspended until the inquiry completed, which meant they spent three months proving that the archive could be trusted while being prohibited from touching the systems that produced it.

Rook faced a formal flight hearing. The review board praised his rescue actions and cited him for unauthorized operational conduct in the same paragraph.

Iven kept the civilian copy of the archive.

Lio lost his command recommendation.

The academy's final finding described him as capable under pressure, strategically inventive, and unsuitable for active fleet leadership pending further review. It did not say whether the pending review would ever end.

He read the finding alone in the residence tower after most of the cadets had gone home for break.

The black pin sat on the desk beside the paper.

He had taken it back from the evidence table after the inquiry. Not because it meant what it had meant at the dinner. Because leaving it behind would have made the choice too clean.

The academy could strip his recommendation. It could close the door to the fleet. It could preserve the pin as evidence of recruitment or return it as an object that had never officially belonged to anyone.

He picked it up.

The metal was warm from the room.

A message appeared on the academy terminal.

**COMMAND EXERCISE REQUIREMENT UPDATED.**

The academy had added a new line to every command simulation and live-response review.

**STATE THE AUTHORITY. RECORD THE ORDER. ACCEPT REVIEW.**

Below it, the system listed the change as a procedural correction.

Lio read the line again.

It was not justice.

It was not proof that Black Banner had ended. The people who had built it still occupied offices, advisory boards, contractor networks, and quiet channels no report could fully map. Anik had disappeared into the kind of protection the program had promised its recruits. The need that produced him remained: the belief that institutions moved too slowly, that emergencies justified hidden authority, that useful people should not have to wait for permission to save lives.

The line was only a beginning.

Lio set the pin beside the terminal.

For the first time, he did not wonder whether someone was watching him make the choice.

The choice was already recorded.

That would have to be enough.
