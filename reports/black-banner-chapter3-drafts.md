# Black Banner Academy: Chapter 3 Drafts By Option

## Option 1: The Covert Recruitment Pipeline

### Chapter 3 - A Better Kind Of Alone

The transfer order arrived through official academic channels, which was the first thing about Black Banner that genuinely frightened Lio.

The black envelope had been theater. The dead scanner, the basement, Anik's folders full of other people's ruin: all of it had the deniable texture of things that could be made never to have happened. But the document glowing on his slate at 0600 was real academy paperwork. Routing codes. Registrar seal. Wren's authorization string, valid and verifiable.

CADET ARDEN, L.

REASSIGNED: ADVANCED INTEGRATED STUDIES.

EFFECTIVE IMMEDIATELY. STANDARD ROTATIONS SUSPENDED.

A secret that could produce its own legitimate paperwork was not a conspiracy. It was an organ.

Across the room, Iven read his own slate upside down over a breakfast ration he was ignoring. "Advanced Integrated Studies." He said it the way he said everything he intended to look up later. "That's not in the catalog."

"It's new."

"New this term?"

"New this week, apparently."

Iven's eyes came up. Iven had a way of looking at people that suggested he was checking their citations. "They pulled you out of Fleet Logistics. You're four points from top of section."

"They pulled Quell out of Constitutional Command." Lio kept his voice bored. "And Sato out of flight rotation, which I'm told caused actual grief counseling."

"Sato." Iven set down his fork. "They put you in a program with Rook Sato."

"Integrated. It's right there in the name."

"Integrated with what?"

Speak of this to no one. The rule had been easy to keep for eleven days because there had been nothing to speak of. Now the nothing had a course code, and Iven Marr, who had once filed a formal correction to the academy's own founding charter display, was going to look under it.

Lio thought of the folder in the basement. Unauthorized access to restricted constitutional crisis archives. Use of a false instructor credential. If Iven started pulling at Black Banner's paperwork, he would do it the way he did everything: thoroughly, on the record, and with his own access history trailing behind him like blood in water.

"With whatever the superintendent wants his favorites integrated with," Lio said. "Command grooming. Photogenic cadets for the recruitment feeds. You know how it works."

"You hate how it works."

"I hate it less when it's me."

He made himself grin when he said it. The grin was the lie; the sentence, unhelpfully, was not.

Iven studied him a moment longer, then went back to his ration. "Well. Congratulations." He said it plainly, without envy, which was worse than envy. "They finally noticed."

Lio spent the walk to Sublevel Nine deciding that the conversation had gone well, and most of the day understanding that it hadn't.

---

The room had changed.

The round table was gone. In its place: a full operations floor in miniature: plot table, six crew stations, a command ring with real fleet-standard interfaces instead of the pedagogical versions upstairs that flashed helpful amber when you were about to do something interesting. The coffee service had survived the renovation. Someone had used it.

"You're late," Rook said, from the depth of a crew couch. "By forty seconds. I know because Vale here has been narrating."

"I've been calibrating," Senn said, without looking up from the station they had already half disassembled. "The interface latency is wrong."

"Wrong how?" Lio asked.

"Wrong as in absent. Academy trainers add ninety milliseconds of artificial lag so cadets don't develop reflexes real ships can't honor." Senn turned a panel toward him: a diagnostic tree Lio didn't recognize. "This has none. This is fleet operational hardware."

Quell stood at the plot table, arms folded. "It's surplus."

"It's current issue."

"Then it's a very good training budget."

"It's a budget," Senn agreed, "that doesn't appear in the academy's published accounts. I checked before breakfast."

The door opened before Quell could decide whether to be reassured. Commander Wren entered first, field coat again, a slate under her arm. Anik followed with the relaxed pace of a man arriving at his own dinner table.

"Stations," Wren said.

They moved. Lio noticed how: no shuffling, no glancing. The four of them distributed themselves around the plot table as if they'd drilled it. Eleven days ago they had been strangers who distrusted each other in different dialects.

"Today is not an ethics problem," Wren said. "Today you work." She touched the slate, and the plot table bloomed with a system map: a mid-transit relief scenario, a civilian station bleeding atmosphere, a support convoy nine hours out, and a threat picture that made everyone lean in at the same time.

"Cadet Arden holds the command ring," Wren said. "Sato, small craft. Vale, systems and traffic. Quell, you are the lawful authority on scene: station governor's office. The exercise ends when the station's population is safe, dead, or you are relieved for cause."

Rook cracked his knuckles. "And the enemy?"

"There is no enemy," Anik said, from beside the coffee. "There is weather, physics, panic, three jurisdictions, and a docking queue. You will find these are worse."

It took them six hours.

Afterward, Lio could not have said when the exercise stopped feeling like a test and started feeling like the first genuinely honest thing the academy had ever asked him to do. Somewhere around the second hour, when the station's rotational section failed and Rook flew four evacuation runs through debris that changed vector every ninety seconds, calling his margins in a voice with no performance left in it. Somewhere around the fourth, when Senn rerouted the docking queue through a maintenance protocol nobody upstairs would have graded as legal, and looked at Lio first — not for permission, for confirmation — and Lio gave it, and it held.

Somewhere around the moment Quell, playing the governor, refused his evacuation order in front of everyone.

"Your order exceeds your authority," she said, flat and formal across the plot table. "Station sovereignty is intact. Convince me or route around me, Commander, but you don't get to pretend I'm not here."

The old Lio, the Lio of eleven days ago, would have heard the room deciding he'd been put in his place. Instead he heard the problem. She wasn't blocking him. She was making him better, and she was doing it because she assumed he could take it.

"Governor," he said. "Your station has ninety minutes of rotation left and my convoy has berths for sixty percent of your population. I can't overrule you. But I can tell you which sixty percent boards if you make me choose fast instead of well."

The pause that followed was Quell thinking, actually thinking, behind the governor's borrowed face.

"Proposal accepted," she said. "Boarding priority is mine. Flight plan is yours."

At the end, the plot table showed a station empty and dead and a convoy heavy with everyone who had been aboard it. FATALITIES: 41. Lio stared at the number and waited for the familiar amphitheater arithmetic: the muttering, the Vesta logic, the room deciding what his answer proved about where he came from.

"Forty-one," Wren said. "Fleet solved this scenario in seventy-three, the year it happened."

Rook's head came around. "It's real?"

"Everything down here is real," Anik said. "That is the curriculum."

He walked the length of the plot table, unhurried, and stopped across from Lio. When he spoke it was to all of them, but he did not look away from Lio while he did it.

"Upstairs, you are being trained to be interchangeable. That is not an insult; a fleet requires it. Any competent officer into any competent slot." He tapped the table once, over the dead station. "Down here you are not interchangeable. Cadet Sato flew that debris field because no one else in this academy can. Cadet Vale bent that docking queue because no one else would have seen it bend. Cadet Quell held sovereign authority against a faster answer, which is the rarest skill in this room. And Cadet Arden—"

"Chose fast instead of well," Lio said.

"Chose well," Anik said, "at speed, in front of people, without needing them to forgive him for it." He let that sit. "You have spent three years being tolerated, Cadet. Notice how quickly you've gotten used to being needed. Notice it honestly. It's the most important data you'll collect down here."

The terrible thing was that he was right, and the more terrible thing was that being told the flattery was flattery did not make it stop working.

Wren dismissed them. At the door, Lio looked back once. She was standing over the plot table, the dead station still glowing under her hands, and she was watching Anik with the expression of someone counting exits.

---

They rode the service lift up together, the four of them, and nobody spoke. It was the good kind of silence, the kind Lio had watched flight crews share in the mess for three years without ever once being inside it.

Rook broke it at Sublevel Two. "Same time tomorrow?"

"Every day," Senn said. "The schedule's blocked through end of term."

"Good," Rook said, and then, aggrieved by his own sincerity: "I mean. Acceptable."

Quell almost smiled. Lio saw it happen and saw her decide to allow it.

The doors opened on the residence level, on floor polish and honor boards and the ordinary bright evening noise of eight hundred cadets who did not know Sublevel Nine existed, and the silence changed instantly into four people not looking at each other.

That was the design, Lio thought. Not the hardware, not the real scenario, not even Anik's surgical praise. This. The lift doors as an airlock between the place where they mattered and the place where they were students. Black Banner didn't have to demand loyalty. It just had to become the only room where any of them could breathe.

Knowing the design was supposed to be his advantage.

He kept telling himself that all the way to his quarters, where Iven was waiting up with the lights on and a slate full of registrar records open on the desk.

"Advanced Integrated Studies," Iven said, "has no syllabus, no instructor of record, no assigned classroom, and a course code from a numbering block the academy retired nine years ago."

Lio stopped in the doorway.

Later, he would have time to inventory everything that hung in that moment. The folder in the basement with Iven's name on the first page. Senn's voice in the corridor: If he warns him, that becomes part of the assessment. Anik's verbs, laid out like instruments. Destroy. Bury. Warn. Protect.

And under all of it, small and ugly as a splinter: six hours of being needed, and the cold that came off the thought of losing it.

"You pulled the registrar records," Lio said.

"Of course I pulled the registrar records. My roommate got reassigned to a course that doesn't exist. I have questions. That's not suspicion, that's literacy."

"Iven." Lio shut the door. "Stop."

"Stop what? Reading public records?"

"Stop pulling threads on this. Tonight. All of it."

Iven leaned back slowly in the desk chair. Iven Marr, who wrote essays on civilian supremacy at two in the morning for no audience but his own conscience, who believed rules were load-bearing and checked them for rot because he loved the building. Lio watched him arrive, with terrible inevitability, at exactly the wrong conclusion.

"You're in something," Iven said quietly. "And you can't talk about it. That's fine. Genuinely fine, Lio. Half of command track ends up under some nondisclosure eventually. But if the paperwork is this bad, then somebody built it badly on purpose, which means the secrecy isn't protecting a program, it's protecting a person, and cadets under bad paperwork are the ones who get spent when it surfaces. Let me help. I'm good at this. I won't touch you. I'll go through the records office, formal inquiry, my name on it. Clean."

His name on it.

The formal inquiry would take nine days to route. His access history would be reviewed within one. And somewhere in a basement, a man in an unmemorable brown suit already had a folder that would end Iven Marr's career in a single referral, a folder that existed, Lio understood now with real nausea, precisely for this. Not to test what Lio would do with a friend's violation. To be there, later, whenever the friend became inconvenient.

They hadn't been assessing his ethics at all. They'd been showing him the hostage.

"No," Lio said.

"Lio—"

"There's nothing to find. It's a superintendent's pet cohort with lazy paperwork, and if you file an inquiry, the only thing you'll accomplish is telling every officer who reviews it that Iven Marr thinks his roommate getting picked over him is a constitutional crisis."

He watched it land. He had aimed it to land at the one soft place Iven kept behind the citations, the fear of being the cadet who footnoted other people's careers because he didn't have one of his own. It was the kind of shot you could only make with three years of friendship for a targeting system.

Iven's face didn't crumple. It did something quieter and much worse. It became polite.

"Right," he said. He closed the registrar records, one window at a time, deliberately, the way he did everything. "Congratulations again. On being noticed."

He took his slate and his half-finished essay to the library, and the door closed, and it was very quiet.

Lio sat on the edge of his bunk in the empty room where, eleven days ago, he had held a black envelope and decided not to wake his friend. The room's night cycle came on and painted the walls low amber. Upstairs, eight hundred cadets slept inside a school they believed in. Nine levels down, there was a plot table where he was necessary, where nobody said Vesta logic, where the coffee was real and the scenarios were real and Amara Quell argued with him like an equal and it had taken Black Banner exactly one working day to make that room feel more like home than this one.

He had protected Iven. He was almost sure he had protected Iven.

He had also just, without one order being given, done Anik's work for him: cut the last line between himself and anyone outside the program, and been paid for it in belonging.

All his life the academy had made him lonely in a crowd, and he had survived it by deciding loneliness was evidence of his own clear sight. Black Banner had studied that survival strategy, priced it, and made its offer: not friendship, which he would have distrusted, but usefulness, which he had wanted so long he'd stopped noticing the wanting.

A better kind of alone. Curated. Load-bearing. Shared with exactly four people and one man in a brown suit who collected wounds the way other men collected debts.

Lio lay back on the bunk without undressing and stared at the ceiling.

"Find out who else is being tested," he'd said, in the corridor, eleven days ago, like a plan.

He was starting to understand the answer was going to be: everyone. Including the version of himself who kept score of how good the basement felt.

Especially him.
